# PACT game experience and comedy ideas

**Draft for discussion, 2 October 2026.** Prepared for the founder after the
[fresh Mosslight session](gamer-perspective-playtest-20261002.md). These are design
ideas, not an implementation assignment, approved specification change or new
ticket batch. No game code, economy, assets or tracker state changed for this report.

My preferred direction is a warm, slightly shabby kingdom whose people take
diplomacy enormously seriously while behaving like complete idiots. Give players
a place they recognize, a plan they understand, a companion they remember and
consequences they want to talk about. Music, animation and ridiculous characters
should make those consequences more expressive. The first-session choices and
return rhythm need attention alongside the comedy.

## What the playtest suggests

In roughly fifteen minutes, Mosslight completed Farm II and Mine II, published a
notice and sent one contact request. The production improvements were real, but
their presentation was subdued. Contact attempts failed before ordinary Silver
accumulated. The successful request remained unanswered. The strongest hook was
the possibility of meeting someone; the weakest stretch was watching two timers.
Luna's incorrect production conversion also made its account of progress harder
to trust. This single assisted session informs the proposals below; it does not
establish how active human diplomacy or a competitive season feels.

The existing [product brief](../../pact/product.md) already specifies a compact
illustrated settlement, optional ambience and event sounds, recognizable Hall
development, and result cards. The [Alpha scope](../../pact/alpha.md) postponed
bespoke art, sound and animation. Much of the proposal develops that established
direction with a stronger personality. Decorative court characters below are
presentation characters; they do not introduce the autonomous NPC population
excluded by the product brief.

## Give the kingdom a personal identity

I would put a small illustrated settlement near the top of Kingdom. It could be
an angled cutaway or a flat toy-town scene: seven recognizable buildings, a Hall
with a crooked banner, fields, a forge, a storehouse and a courtyard. This is a
view of the player's settlement, without geography or movement mechanics.

Farm II adds another patch of crops or a better mill. Mine II adds a second cart
and a new section of the forge. Hall improvements change the settlement silhouette.
Working builders have scaffolding and a visible job site. A completed upgrade
leaves a permanent visual change, so returning to the page feels like returning
to something the player built. The actual game state determines those changes.

The style I have in mind is parchment and ink with warm clay, deep teal, tarnished
brass and small cloth details. Add uneven roof tiles, patched flags, wood grain,
stamped paper and faint coffee rings on the court's desk. Keep texture behind
the scene and decorative panels; quantities and treaty terms stay easy to read.

At arrival, ask a small question: **“What kind of kingdom are you trying to build?”**
Possible ambitions: feed the neighbours, become a reliable trader, build a place
that survives trouble, or become an extremely suspicious accountant. These are
player intentions the Envoy can discuss, without class bonuses or locked playstyles.
A banner colour and a short motto could reinforce that intention.

The kingdom should accumulate a little personal history. Its first completed
farm, first accepted contact and first delivered trade can become keepsakes in a
private Hall scrapbook. A lopsided wooden plaque saying “WE HAVE AGRICULTURE” is
more memorable than a generic success toast. These would be decorative records
of actual milestones; any new reward entitlement needs separate product review.

## Improve the first session and the return

Offer one near-term ambition with a concrete explanation. For the observed
Mosslight starter state, a food plan could say:

> Keep the Home Guard fed and create a surplus we can offer a neighbour.

Show the current food situation, the effect of Farm II and the current next step.
Using the starter's unchanged eight Home Guard and 100% harvest, Farm II changes
gross Grain production from **30 to 45 per hour** and net production after upkeep
from **22 to 37 per hour**. Those figures follow the [rulebook](../../pact/rules.md)
and the observed owner screen. A result card should make that improvement obvious.

During construction, suggest useful existing activities: choose a second
investment, inspect a real notice, write a public introduction, explore a Court
when one exists, or simply leave with a clear return expectation. Decorative
character interactions can add amusement, but every wait should not require
repeated clicking or collecting production manually.

The return screen should answer three questions quickly: **What changed? What
matters to my goal? What can I decide now?** For Mosslight, it could show both
completed upgrades, the higher income, and “Waiting for Juniper Reach to accept.”
Then offer a different useful choice or a comfortable stopping point. Repeating
“Meet a real player” after an outgoing request feels disconnected from the story.

After the common first Farm II, frame the second builder as a choice with a
purpose. A Mine supports future construction; a Market improves ordinary Silver
income for diplomacy and other costs; a Wall strengthens home defence. Put the
actual costs, timing and effect beside each option. Ask which ambition matters
today. The identity comes from choosing among existing tradeoffs and seeing the
result, rather than an Envoy quietly queueing the same two buildings every time.

A pending request can have a visible letter in the courier's outbox. Its status
stays honest: sent, awaiting the recipient's human, accepted or expired. Show
notice age and expiry, and treat a kingdom's active status separately from human
availability. Explore an optional player-written response expectation rather
than exposing private online state or promising a reply.

I would first explore presentation and the existing free accepted-invitation
route before changing cold-contact prices or starting grants. If the opening
still stalls, a different first-contact mechanic is a separate balance proposal
requiring specification and playtest work. Decorative improvements cannot supply
a missing human counterparty.

## A small cast of ridiculous court officials

Choose one memorable lead character and two supporting characters initially.
Give each a recognizable silhouette, a few poses and recurring habits. My
favourite lead is **Sir Honk, Auditor of Grain**.

| Character | Visual idea | Personality and example line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sir Honk | Goose in an enormous brass visor, dragging a ledger bigger than himself | Obsessive about supplies. “I have counted the Grain. Emotionally, I disagree with the total.” |
| Pebble | Tiny stone foreman with a huge hard hat and an even larger clipboard | Permanently proud of construction. “We have built an additional farm. Previous farm has been informed.” |
| Moth | Dishevelled courier carrying a bag marked IMPORTANT PROBABLY | Confident about sending letters, honest about waiting. “Delivered to their inbox. Acceptance remains someone else's problem.” |
| Professor Bean | Wizard whose hat contains an abacus and whose wand is a ruler | Dry, nerdy strategist. “The harvest schedule remains unmoved by our optimism.” |

These characters can react to facts and decorate the player's world. They do not
negotiate, sign, reveal information or decide the player's actions. A character's
joke appears alongside a clear factual status. The connected model remains the
Envoy.

Let the player occasionally interact with the art: poke the banner and it gets
stuck on the Hall; click Pebble's helmet and a much smaller helmet falls out;
Sir Honk produces a quill, then an absurdly longer quill. These are brief local
gags, with no resource rewards, daily chore or inference call attached.

An optional presentation tone could offer **calm**, **dry** and **chaotic**.
Tone changes the flavour lines, voice clips and visual reactions while the
factual explanation and controls remain consistent. The joke should usually be
on the court's bureaucracy, not on a player struggling to understand the game.

## Animation and comedy tied to actual events

Short event reactions can make the player remember outcomes. Routine production
should remain calm; the large reactions belong to meaningful changes.

| Confirmed event or current state | Animation idea | Sound or line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First Farm II completion | Scaffolding drops, crops stand up, Pebble proudly raises a tiny pennant | Brief wooden clack and a badly played trumpet. “AGRICULTURE HAS BEEN UPGRADED.” |
| Mine completion | A cart emerges triumphantly, loses one wheel, and is immediately repaired as a visual gag | Cart rattle followed by one confident metal ping |
| Human contact acceptance | Moth bursts through a paper envelope, dusts himself off and bows | Paper flutter. “Another kingdom has acknowledged our existence.” |
| First actual trade delivery | A tiny cart carries a conspicuously tiny parcel; the displayed amount stays exact | A little bell. “A dignified teaspoon of commerce.” |
| Completed two-party signature | Two seals press the actual agreement; Sir Honk adds a useless decorative third stamp | Two firm stamp sounds. “The paperwork has become legally heavier.” |
| Contact still awaiting acceptance | Moth sits on the outgoing letter and slowly knits a scarf | Usually silent. “Still awaiting a human reply.” |
| Insufficient ordinary Silver | Sir Honk opens a purse and finds lint; a grey moth escapes | Tiny deflated squeak. “Construction budget cannot finance friendship.” |
| Raid warning | Sir Honk drops the ledger and performs a very short operatic honk | Low drums and an optional absurd vocal flourish, alongside the clear warning |
| Contract default | The court clerk removes the celebratory ribbon and places it in a sad little drawer | A muted snapped cord; show the actual default consequences plainly |
| Full storehouse | A barrel lid lifts and a decorative cabbage rolls out | Occasional visual joke; no sound on every production tick |

For the screaming idea: an **optional, roughly half-second opera-goose panic**
when a real raid warning first appears would be wonderful. It should have a
recognizable setup and happen rarely. Blasting a scream on every failed command
would quickly make ordinary play exhausting. Repeated failures should produce
useful guidance and quiet reactions.

Small contrasts make the humour work: a magnificent fanfare for a tiny parcel;
a very solemn seal ceremony witnessed by a goose; a foreman celebrating a wall
with a speech longer than the wall. Use a handful of recurring jokes rather than
an unrelated meme for every button.

## Music and sound direction

I imagine a small medieval ensemble that occasionally goes slightly wrong:
plucked strings, a warm woodwind, light hand percussion, a wooden xylophone and
one brass player with dangerous levels of confidence. The core melody should be
pleasant enough to stay in the background. The comedy comes from short accents.

Possible original music briefs:

- **The Kingdom Is Probably Fine:** a calm, gently bouncing settlement theme with
  plucked strings, woodwind and soft workshop taps. A short looping composition,
  with some breathing space, would suit ordinary planning.
- **Diplomacy in Very Small Shoes:** a restrained court variation with parchment
  shuffles, a bass pluck and a slightly pompous melody. Suitable for People.
- **The Goose Has Concerns:** a sparse tension variation with low drums and a
  nervous woodwind phrase. It responds only to threats the owner is allowed to see.
- **A Historically Significant Turnip:** a two-second achievement flourish that
  begins grandly and ends with a tiny kazoo-like note.

Small effects should be pleasant on their own: a paper fold for grouped messages,
a seal press for a signature, a first-delivery chime, a warm build-completion knock,
and soft forge ambience. Wind, cloth flutter and an occasional courtyard chicken
can give the scene life without every resource tick making a noise.

Offer an explicit Enable sound control and independent music, effects, ambience
and voice volumes, consistent with the product brief. A calm mode can remove
vocal gags. Reduced-motion and silent presentations retain the same information.
Humour should accompany serious losses without covering their consequences or
requiring the player to hear or watch an effect to understand them.

## Generated art and animation ideas

Generated assets could help explore the style without committing to a huge asset
set. I would begin with a shared style sheet: palette, outline weight, perspective,
lighting and the court's deliberately mismatched equipment. Then explore one
settlement, one character and one event before multiplying the variations.

Useful asset concepts include a parchment background, patched cloth banners,
wood-and-copper building details, character pose sheets and tiny illustrated
Chronicle keepsakes. Sir Honk needs idle, pleased, suspicious and panic poses.
Pebble needs idle, working and victory poses. Small reusable motions—blinking,
quill movement, a wing flap, a banner sway—can make a few illustrations lively.

For an exploratory generation brief:

> A tiny medieval office inhabited by an anxious goose accountant, a proud stone
> foreman and a scruffy courier. Warm parchment, deep teal, clay red and tarnished
> brass. Hand-drawn ink outlines, patched equipment, readable silhouettes and
> affectionate absurdity. A settlement that looks loved, improvised and functional.

For looping animation, consistent key poses and a few deliberately composed
frames are more useful than many unrelated generated clips. Inspect character
consistency, transparency edges and the loop join. Keep the informative part of
the UI in ordinary text, and keep public decorative portraits independent of
another player's hidden buildings or military state. No asset generation is part
of this proposal.

Seasonal details could deepen the atmosphere later: own fields becoming a little
drier as the shared harvest multiplier falls, an increasingly worried goose
checking the food ledger, and a quieter version of the settlement theme. Tie
those cues to the actual public season and the owner's authorized condition.
They can make the existing six-week pressure tangible without adding surprise
disasters or silently changing production.

## The Envoy as a companion

The interesting relationship is still between the human and their Envoy. Let a
human objective remain visibly present across the game's reporting surface:
“A peaceful food supplier,” “A cautious builder,” or the player's own wording.
The human can revise it at any time; an ambition is not permission to start runs
or exceed authority.

Each briefing should put a few exact facts before the interpretation: what
completed, the production effect in consistent units, what is waiting, and the
available choices. Then add a short explanation in the chosen voice. For example:

> Farm II is complete. Food production rose from 30 to 45 per hour. With the
> same Home Guard, that leaves 37 per hour before exports. We can build reserves
> while waiting for Juniper Reach's human to answer.
>
> Sir Honk has upgraded our food outlook from “concerning” to “professionally
> suspicious.”

The exact quantities and waiting state should come from authorized game facts.
Character lines can use a small event-specific library; a new model call is not
needed every time the browser refreshes or a bell rings. A player's next bounded
Envoy run is still an explicit player choice.

## A concrete version of the opening

Mosslight begins as a small settlement with a crooked flag. The human names it
and chooses “feed the neighbours” as an ambition. Sir Honk looks into the grain
bin, consults an outrageously long ledger and points to a clear food overview.
The first Farm II choice explains the expected improvement. The Envoy can carry
out the authorized queue; Pebble begins visibly working at that farm.

While waiting, the page offers a public introduction and a few actual notices.
If ordinary Silver is insufficient, it explains the contact wait once, gives the
current return estimate and offers the existing invitation route when relevant.
Moth keeps the player's outgoing letter in view. Nobody pretends a reply exists.

When the farm actually completes, the field changes, Pebble raises the terrible
pennant, and the result card shows the gain. One ridiculous trumpet note sounds
if enabled. The Hall gets a tiny “WE HAVE AGRICULTURE” keepsake. The next decision
connects to the chosen food ambition rather than automatically proposing another
timer. If a human eventually accepts, Moth delivers that new fact with a flourish.

That sequence would give the first session a place, a goal, a wait, a payoff and
an anticipated relationship. The actual social outcome remains uncertain, which
is where authentic player stories can begin.

## Which ideas I would explore first

My first candidate is a small **Farm II celebration** concept: one settlement
illustration with an observable upgrade change, Sir Honk and Pebble, a clear
before/after result card, one calm loop and a few event sounds. Pair it with better
pending-contact guidance and an exact Envoy briefing. This tests whether the
core accomplishment becomes more memorable without requiring a complete art set.

Next, explore actual first-delivery and contact-acceptance moments with a small
real group over several visits. Add the courier and private Hall memories if
players respond to the first concept. More characters, seasonal scenery, richer
music and additional cosmetic variety can follow the evidence rather than
preceding it. These are suggested exploration priorities, not activated Later work.

The questions I would use to judge it are simple: Can players explain why they
built something? Do they remember their kingdom and a character? Can they tell
what changed? Do they return voluntarily? Do they care who answers their letter?
Are the jokes still amusing on a later visit, and is the quiet version equally
understandable? A useful signal would be someone telling a small story about an
actual choice or another player, alongside enjoying the goose.

The feeling I would aim for is: **“I want to check on my slightly ridiculous little
kingdom and see whether that other person answered.”**
