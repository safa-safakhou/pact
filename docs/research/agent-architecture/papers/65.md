## 65 — Human-Level Performance in First-Person Multiplayer Games with Population-Based Deep Reinforcement Learning

**Source/coverage:** Jaderberg et al., [arXiv v1, July 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.01281v1). Read main text pp. 1–10 and supplementary methods/captions pp. 17–42; reference section and supplement opening only partial. Equations, figures and videos not independently audited.

**Mechanism:** Pixel-based action policy with fast/slow recurrent representations, stochastic latent variables and external memory. Population training evolves reward mappings and hyperparameters while agents learn through play. Temporal hierarchy does not specify explicit goals or skills.

**Evidence:** Modified Quake capture-the-flag, unseen generated maps, teammates and team sizes; human tournaments and ablations support combined method. Sparse win-only training failed. Added temporal layers beyond two brought little benefit.

**Limits/contrary evidence:** Thirty-agent population; two billion steps per agent, 1,920 concurrent arenas. New maps are within the same game. Event reward structure supplies prior information. Mechanical accuracy/reaction advantages affect human comparisons. Professional testers learned a consistently winning exploit on one fixed map after six hours, though changing maps remained difficult. Representation probes establish decodability, not causal strategic understanding.

**Mission implication — inference:** Strong real-time mechanics and extended coordination can be learned together without a separate symbolic planner. Memory, training diversity and reward design matter alongside architecture; degradation under controller failure was not demonstrated.
