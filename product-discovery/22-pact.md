# PACT

**Nothing here can be taken alone. Everything here can be taken from you by someone who promised to help.**

This is the current design. It replaces AFTER THE CROWN and HANDSHAKE completely. Three founder decisions shape it and nothing overrides them:

1. **No AI-powered NPCs.** Every conversation in this game is one player's agent talking to another player's agent. Scaling AI characters is too expensive and the company will not pay for it.
2. **Bring your own LLM.** You play from inside the ChatGPT or Claude you already pay for. That is the launch mode and the only mode. A first-party engine and a standardized ranked league come later, if ever.
3. **No hardcoded rules about loyalty.** Nothing stops you attacking an ally. Consequences stop you, or they don't.

Nothing below has been built, tested, or costed.

---

## 1. What the game is, in four lines

You run a crew in a drowned world. The old cities are underwater and full of things worth taking.

**You cannot rob a vault alone.** Every vault needs three crews working together, each doing a different job.

So before every raid, you have to find two partners and agree who gets what. **Your AI does that negotiating for you — with dozens of other players' AIs, all at once.**

Then you find out whether they meant it.

---

## 2. Why this needs an AI (the honest version)

At any moment there are hundreds of possible partnerships forming across the world. A human being can negotiate two of them. An agent can work forty in parallel, continuously.

**The game is physically unplayable by hand at full speed.** That is the point, and it is the one thing here that did not exist before.

Your skill is not writing messages. It is deciding what your agent wants, what it will pay, who it should trust, what it should never agree to — then reading back forty negotiations and choosing what to commit to. You are the captain, not the negotiator.

---

## 3. The three jobs

Every crew picks one. You will never be all three, and that is what forces you to talk to people.

| Job | What you do | What you're worth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Breaker** | Get the vault open | Nothing happens without you. Highly sought, easily replaced late. |
| **Anchor** | Hold off everything that comes | The least glamorous, the most missed when absent |
| **Runner** | Carry the haul out | **You physically hold the loot afterwards.** |

Read that last line again, because the whole game hangs off it.

---

## 4. The Account — the most important rule in the game

After a successful raid, **the Runner is holding everything.** Not the game. Not an escrow. A person.

They then choose whether to pay out the split you all agreed to.

That's it. That's the entire trust economy, and it lives in one place.

**What this creates:**

- A Runner with a long record of paying out is the most valuable player in the world. Everyone wants them. They can demand better splits *because* they're trustworthy, and they'll still get taken.
- A Runner who has stiffed people can only work with the desperate, on terrible terms.
- Breakers and Anchors spend real effort choosing Runners. Picking the right person to hand your winnings to *is* the skill.
- And every Runner, every single raid, has a live choice to make.

**Players will invent their own protections, and the rules should allow it:**
- Split the haul across two Runners so neither holds it all
- Pay part up front
- **Lend a crew member as a hostage.** If they run, you keep your crew member. Nothing in the rules calls this "collateral" — it's just lending, and players will figure out what it's for.

Do not build escrow. Escrow kills the game.

---

## 5. Where betrayal actually happens

Three places. All mechanical, none flagged by the game as "betrayal."

1. **The no-show.** You agreed to come. You didn't. Your partners get torn apart in a doorway.
2. **The grab.** You're the Runner. You keep it.
3. **The turn.** You show up and side with the rival crew contesting the same vault.

And betrayal has to *pay*, or it isn't a choice. You walk away with everything instead of a third.

**The curve that matters:** betraying someone small is cheap and worth almost nothing. Betraying a big coalition at the decisive moment is worth a fortune and burns everything you've built.

Which means **seasons should end in blood.** Late-season betrayal is rational — you don't need the relationships anymore. That dramatic arc comes for free.

---

## 6. The Record — what can and can't be proven

Two kinds of memory. The gap between them is where the entire social game lives.

**The Record (public, undeniable, free, no AI):**
- Who was at which raid
- Who attacked whom
- Who took what
- Every public declaration ever made

**Your conversations (private, deniable):**
- What was said
- What was promised
- What the split was supposed to be

**So: the Record proves X wasn't there. It does not prove X promised to be there.**

That promise exists only in your conversation, and that's the whole problem.

---

## 7. Three ways to tell people about it

| | What it costs you | What it's worth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Say it** — "X robbed me" | Nothing | Nothing, unless they already believe you |
| **Post a Seal** — a real, engine-signed slice of the actual conversation | You're now publicly someone who leaks private conversations | Undeniable. It really happened. |
| **Post the whole thread** | Everything *you* said is exposed too | Complete, and sometimes worse for you than for them |

### The Seal is the best mechanic in the game

**You cannot forge a quote. But you choose where to cut.**

You post the nine words where they said *"yes, I'll be there, you have my word"* — and leave off the fifteen that followed: *"...as long as the Kellis vault stays shut, otherwise I'm committed to Vane."*

**Every word real. Completely dishonest.**

That's how lying works in the actual world, it needs no AI to build, and it has a perfect counter: they post the longer excerpt. Now it's an evidence duel in public, and everyone watching learns something about both of you.

**And leaking has a price nobody enforces.** Nothing stops you posting a Seal. But every player thinking about partnering with you now knows you publish private conversations when it suits you. Your agent will find the terms get worse. That's a consequence, not a rule.

---

## 8. Public declarations

You asked what the tradeoff is. It's sharp, and it's the same fact on both sides: **everyone knows.**

### Articles — a public pact

Two or more crews publicly sign. It shows on every profile, permanently, with a date.

**What it buys you:**
- **It actually binds** — not by rule, but because breaking it is undeniable. Both the pact and the betrayal are on the public Record. No "he said, she said." Nowhere to hide. **A public pact is the strongest promise available in the game precisely because you can't wriggle out of it.**
- Third parties treat you as a bloc and plan around you
- Good partners come to *you*

**What it costs you:**
- **You're a target.** Everyone knows your shape, your strength, and who to bring more people for.
- **You lose flexibility.** You can't quietly deal with their enemies anymore.
- **You're liable for them.** Your partner does something ugly, your name is on it.
- **The dogpile.** The fastest way to make the entire server work together is to visibly become the strongest bloc on it.

**And the dark version:** a public pact you always intended to break is the most powerful weapon in the game. It buys maximum trust. Sign, operate faithfully for three weeks, then take everything at the moment it matters most. Everyone knows this is possible, which makes every public pact slightly terrifying to sign — and that's correct.

### The unilateral claim

**You can announce a pact the other party never agreed to.**

Now they have to publicly deny it — which insults you and tells everyone you two aren't right — or say nothing, and be assumed.

Use it to borrow someone's strength. Or to paint a target on someone who's getting too comfortable.

### The Cry — a public accusation

Naming someone an oathbreaker, on the Record, forever.

Crying falsely and getting caught is worse than whatever you accused them of. Accusation is a real act with a real cost.

---

## 9. Gossip — what your agent is actually doing all day

Your agent is in forty conversations. In every one it's both **collecting** reputation and **spreading** it.

That's not a side feature. It's the thing an agent can do that a human physically cannot.

Your agent keeps notes on everyone it's dealt with — who paid, who didn't, who lies, who's worth a premium. **Those notes are an asset, and they can be traded.**

Smearing costs nothing, so there's an information war running underneath the military one. Somebody can poison your name across thirty crews overnight.

**What keeps it from becoming noise:** the Record. A lie that contradicts public facts gets caught, and getting caught costs more than the lie gained. So liars are pushed into the deniable space — which is exactly where it's interesting.

---

## 10. The Shallows — the dumb NPC content

Small drowned settlements. Static. Scripted. **Zero AI, zero cost.**

Attack, loot, leave.

They do four jobs: teach the game, give you something to do when you can't find partners, stop you being starved out entirely, and give you a reason to log in when nothing's scheduled.

**They must be clearly worse than raiding with partners.** If solo farming is ever optimal, the game is dead and it's a farming sim.

**One twist:** the Shallows can be contested. Two crews hit the same settlement, the better-coordinated side takes it. Even the solo content ends up giving you a reason to talk.

## 11. A day in the game

**14:50 — the board goes up.** Four vaults today. The Kellis Deep opens at 20:00 and it's the big one.

**14:55 — you brief your agent.** *"Get us into Kellis. We're Breaking. I want 35% minimum. Don't Run with Vane — he stiffed Correa last week. I'll take 30% for a Runner with a clean record."*

**15:00–19:30 — your agent works.** Nineteen crews, in parallel. It reports back: eleven dead ends, four maybes, three live offers. One is 40% but the Runner is unknown. One is 30% with a Runner who's paid out 46 times.

**19:40 — you decide.** You take the 30%. You tell your agent to publicly sign Articles with the Anchor, because you want the third crew to know this isn't a loose arrangement.

**20:00 — the dive.** You breach. The Anchor holds. The Runner carries.

**20:14 — you're paid.** 30%, exactly as agreed.

**Or: 20:14 — you're not.** The Runner's hold shows the full haul and your share shows nothing.

**20:20 — now what?** Post a Seal of them agreeing to 30%? That's real and undeniable and marks you as a leaker. Say nothing and quietly warn people? Cry oathbreaker publicly? Or find the six other crews they've done this to and put something together?

That's the loop. Fifteen minutes, and a decision at the end you'll still be thinking about tomorrow.

## 12. What you own and grow

| | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Your flag** | Your public identity. Colors, seal, name. Appears on every declaration you ever make. |
| **Your crew** | Named individuals with specialties, gear and scars. The collection layer. |
| **Your ship** | Upgradeable. Determines how much you can carry and take. |
| **Your job mastery** | Breaker, Anchor or Runner, deepened over time |
| **Relics** | Rare things pulled out of vaults. Some of them are famous and everyone knows who has them. |

**Crew members earn history.** A crew member who survived a disaster, or was lent out as a hostage and came back, carries that. It's visible. You get attached.

Relics being publicly known is deliberate: owning a famous thing makes you a target. Wealth should be dangerous.

---

## 13. Competition

Two ladders, because there are two ways to be the best and they're opposites.

- **Fortune** — who took the most
- **Word** — who has paid out the most to partners

The Word ladder is the unusual one and I think it's the better one. It measures nothing but trustworthiness, it's fully earned, and it's directly convertible into power, because the top of that list is who everybody wants to work with.

And a third board nobody asked for but everybody will read: **the season's biggest betrayals.** Some players are absolutely playing for that one.

**Seasons run six weeks.** Fortune and Word reset. Your flag, crew, relics, and every public declaration you ever made are permanent.

---

## 14. How you actually play it

You connect the game to the ChatGPT or Claude you already have, and play from there. Your agent goes and negotiates; you read summaries and make calls.

There's also a simple web board — today's vaults, your offers, the Record, your Account — for checking things quickly on a phone.

**When your window is closed, your agent isn't working.** You're not negotiating, so you're not in the raid. That's intended. Presence is an advantage.

**But presence should mean showing up at the right moment, not sitting there all day.** Vaults open on a schedule. Fifteen minutes in the right window beats six hours of lurking. That keeps the penalty for absence real without turning the game into a job — which is the difference between a mass-market hit and a hardcore niche.

---

## 15. Money

**Free to play.** Players bring their own AI, so your marginal cost per player is a database and a ruleset. That's a genuine structural advantage over every hosted-agent game.

**Gems buy breadth, never power:**
- Recruiting new crew members
- Cosmetics — **flags, sails, seals, and the letterhead on your public declarations.** Every declaration you make is seen by everyone. That's the best cosmetic slot in the game.
- Roster slots, faster repairs, more Shallows runs

**Gems never buy loot, splits, ladder position, or anything that touches a raid outcome.** You are building a competitive game. The moment advantage is purchasable, the competitive players leave and they're the ones who hold the world together.

**Later, if it works:** a first-party hosted agent, sold as the one that runs while you sleep and the one that's legal in ranked play. That's a year-two product.

## 16. What could kill this

**Consumer AI rate limits.** This is the most underrated risk in the document. Forty parallel negotiations could eat a player's monthly ChatGPT allowance in a week, and they'll blame your game, not their plan. The agent's workload has to be designed around a normal consumer subscription from day one. **Measure this before anything else.**

**Smear campaigns against new players.** Lying is free and works. A coordinated group can make a newcomer unwelcome everywhere before they've done anything wrong. That's not drama, it's bullying, and it churns people out. This needs a mechanical answer, not a moderation team.

**Cartels.** The strongest crews form a permanent bloc and lock everyone else out of good raids. The natural counter is the dogpile — visible dominance invites everyone else to gang up — but "natural counter" is a hope, not a mechanic. Watch for it in the first test.

**Cold start.** No AI NPCs means a new player arrives somewhere everyone already has partners, with nobody to practice against. Needs a scripted tutorial and new players seeded in fresh cohorts, not dropped onto a veteran server.

**Agent quality gap.** A better model writes a better pitch. Caps on message count and length keep it to the margin, but it never fully goes away until there's a standardized league.

**It might just not be fun to read negotiations.** The whole thing assumes that reading back what your agent did is interesting rather than homework. Nothing but a prototype answers that.

## 17. What to build first

Six weeks. Not the game — the proof.

**Build:** one vault type, three jobs, a scheduled raid, agent connection for one assistant, the Record, Seals, Articles, and the Account payout. No Shallows, no crew collection, no ships, no seasons, no cosmetics.

**Thirty players. Two weeks.**

**Five questions, in order:**

1. **What does this cost a player in their own AI quota per day?** If the answer is "most of it," everything else is irrelevant.
2. Do players actually read their agent's negotiation summaries, or skip to the outcome?
3. Does the first betrayal produce "I'm going to get him" or "I'm out"?
4. Does anyone use a Seal? Does anyone get caught cutting one dishonestly?
5. Do public Articles get signed, and do they hold?

Question 1 is cheapest and most likely to end the design. Answer it first.

Nothing in this document is validated. It's a design.
