# Fieldwise

**One sentence:** Teach an inventive companion your way of solving problems, then explore places where it must put those lessons to use alongside you.

Fieldwise is the developed form of Apprentice Expedition. It is a complete proposed Version 1.0, not an implemented or tested game. Quantities and targets are design decisions for estimating and evaluating this proposal; audience demand, capability, and production costs remain unvalidated. The founder has requested development of all three finalists before selecting one.

## Identity, insight, and audience

**Name direction:** Fieldwise. Alternatives: Apprentice Expedition, Small Great Things, Trail & Tinker. Names require store, domain, and trademark clearance before commercial use.

**Category:** Single-player expedition puzzle adventure with a teachable autonomous companion. **Platform:** Windows PC on Steam for 1.0, keyboard/mouse and controller. **Audience:** Adults and teens who enjoy thoughtful adventure, expressive problem solving, companions, and building useful systems; no programming or AI-account knowledge required. The intended presentation avoids explicit mature content; a formal age rating must be obtained rather than assumed.

**Core insight:** Players can become attached to a behavior they recognize as partly their own creation. A lesson that works in a new context creates both authorship and surprise. The game should exist if that feeling sustains enjoyable play beyond choosing a conventional companion loadout.

The player is a field restorer returning abandoned scientific gardens to working order. A compact wheeled-and-legged survey companion joins them. The companion is a working partner, not a dependent pet that becomes distressed when ignored. It has a recognizable form, expressive animation, a name, and a history of lessons and expeditions. No romance, simulated emotional dependence, or off-session needs are part of the product.

**Visual and audio direction:** Readable miniature landscapes, warm materials, chunky mechanisms, clear object silhouettes, and visible physical connections. The camera is a rotatable three-quarter view. Human-authored animation, environmental sound, and short nonverbal companion sounds carry personality. Generated text is brief and optional to read; generated speech is outside 1.0. This reduces interruption and preserves a legible visual identity.

Closest comparisons are the taught behavior of *Black & White*, explicit authorship in *Gladiabots* and *Autonauts*, and the satisfaction of a systemic puzzle solution. These are references, not a claim to combine their entire feature sets. The proposed difference is **teaching transferable approaches during an embodied adventure**, rather than programming a factory or selecting a companion's fixed tricks. ## What the human and agent do

The human moves through the site, manipulates their own equipment, selects priorities, discovers methods, gives examples, and makes joint commitments. They are not a spectator or a prompt author at a desk. The agent observes, recognizes usable relationships, chooses a complementary approach, operates its equipment, and adapts when circumstances change.

Both have competent basic movement and different tools. The human carries a tether, a portable brace, and an adjustable coupler. The companion can carry bulky items, anchor itself, and reach small service passages. Neither can directly operate the other's body. Their tools create shared maneuvers: the companion holds a carrier while the human changes a coupling; the human braces a platform while the companion ferries a load.

The human remains important when the model improves because they still author priorities, arrange situations, and perform useful embodied actions. The companion should be allowed to solve work it can reasonably solve. Difficulty must grow through richer joint situations, not arbitrary degradation of agent intelligence.

All bindings are remappable. No operation requires precise dragging, speech, a timed keystroke, or a remembered sentence. Radials can be replaced by a list. Text scale, color-independent state symbols, reduced motion, adjustable camera, subtitles, and hold/toggle options ship in 1.0. Timing assists change challenge records but never remove access to the campaign.

The interface shows the companion's next intended action and the relevant target, not a full reasoning transcript. Before a consequential change, a short preview identifies a cost or conflict: “Use case as support; specimen loses its container.” The game state supplies the cost. An explanation must not be trusted as proof of what internally caused a model choice.

## Major mechanics and rule vocabulary

Fieldwise uses a finite, compositional interaction system. Objects have visible mass classes, support surfaces, coupling points, fragility, and permitted tool connections. Movement paths and collisions are ordinary game systems. Devices implement specific authored relationships: a coupled crank changes a platform; a placed weight holds a release; a carrier can support cargo or become a counterweight if the permitted surfaces fit.

Twelve reusable mechanism families make up the 1.0 vocabulary: carriers, beams, ramps, braces, tethers, couplers, counterweights, lifts, gates, floating supports, wind vanes, and relay switches. Art variants do not count as new mechanics. The exact physical behavior of each family is authored and testable. Language cannot make an unsupported object do something new.

Each expedition has a main restoration/retrieval objective and up to two optional conditions. Optional conditions create tradeoffs between protecting a specimen, spending a limited field tool, using a shorter route, or recovering equipment. Outcomes are recognized from world state. There is no model judge deciding whether a solution “felt clever.”

**Teaching:** The player marks an action or short sequence already performed and identifies its relevant result. The companion offers a compact interpretation and, when useful, two small contextual comparisons. The player can endorse, narrow, or reject that interpretation. The saved lesson records examples, desired relationships, exclusions, and priority relative to other lessons. This is game-specific memory and adaptation; it is not a promise of training model weights.

**Generalization:** Later arrangements change locations, objects, and constraints while preserving some relationships. The agent should apply an approach when the relationship fits and choose another when it does not. The game must avoid teaching exact coordinates and calling their replay intelligence.

**Conflicting lessons:** A direct current instruction overrides a general style preference. Mission restrictions and legal physical actions override both. Two otherwise valid lessons can conflict; before a costly action the companion presents the tradeoff. The player can temporarily prioritize one without erasing the other. One confusing example must not silently rewrite an entire companion.

**Recovery:** Pause, cancel an uncommitted plan, or restore the last local checkpoint. Practice comparisons and invalid model responses consume no field resources. Legitimate player-approved risky actions can damage cargo or consume equipment, but cannot permanently destroy the companion or its history. Difficulty comes from resource tradeoffs and coordination, not punishment for provider faults.

## Session, progression, success, and failure

The repeated loop is **inspect → attempt → teach or revise → undertake together → adapt → review**. A session is one 20–35-minute expedition, with save-anytime support at safe state boundaries and automatic checkpoints before major maneuvers.

Campaign progression opens new mechanism combinations and locations. It does not sell or grind general intelligence. Completing a region's main objectives opens its final site; optional medals never block the story. A companion's useful lesson history accumulates, but any lesson can be suspended or rolled back. Restoring an earlier history does not erase cosmetic rewards.

Success means restoring the site and extracting the required item or signal. Optional distinctions recognize all cargo preserved, equipment recovered, or a specified constraint fulfilled. There is no universal efficiency score that forces all players toward a single approach. Failure means losing the current optional objective, exhausting the site's recoverable resources, or choosing to reset a maneuver. The game explains the cause through the event history.

Retention comes from four sources: finishing the expedition sequence; revisiting sites with new tools and methods; pursuing optional constraints; and trying a friend's challenge seed. A player can finish and feel satisfied. There are no seasons, daily attendance rewards, offline harvesting, or infinite progression bars.

| Companion | One body with name, colors, earned accessories, lesson history, and three reversible profile slots |

| Operations | Online-agent status, crash recovery, local saves plus cloud reconciliation, support tools, abuse limits, telemetry controls, and service disclosure |

Expected first clear is roughly 8–12 hours, with additional challenge play for people who enjoy mastery. That is a pacing target inferred from site counts, not measured playtime. Scope should be reduced coherently before production commitment if a site proves expensive; shipping fewer complete regions with a rewritten ending is preferable to shipping empty regions. After the release specification is locked, required systems cannot be deferred under a prototype label.

**Explicitly outside 1.0:** live multiplayer, a public level editor/catalogue, competitive ladders, off-session operation, generated voices, arbitrary custom-agent execution, personal-account memory import, and a player trading economy. The game is complete without these.

## Five gameplay walkthroughs

**First opening.** Select accessibility preferences, see the online-service explanation, and start without choosing a provider. In the Garden Works opening, move a carrier together and discover a way to use it as support. Mark the successful approach. The companion applies it on a shifted arrangement while you operate the release. Within the first eight minutes the intended payoff is visible: the example changed what the companion tried. A supplied conventional help action is available if needed, but the game does not pretend an authored tutorial solution proves adaptive transfer.

**Typical evening.** Enter a Wind Terraces site with one brace and two optional goals. You demonstrate that preserving a delicate container matters more than reusing it. The companion proposes a route using a spare vane as ballast while you couple the carrier. A shift in wind closes the direct route; you inspect another anchor, redirect the plan, and recover the cargo. At the end you review the one example that mattered, keep the revised lesson, and choose the next site or leave.

**Experienced play.** You train an approach across two positive examples and a counterexample, so it responds to a relationship rather than a familiar prop. On the Observatory capstone, you deliberately make a shared maneuver possible by moving an anchor that the companion cannot reach. It applies the lesson to different cargo. Your contribution is conceptual teaching, arranging the opportunity, and executing the human half.

**Interaction with a friend.** A friend shares a Water Courts challenge with a replay that preserves every specimen. You try the same seed with your lesson profile and use more equipment but preserve a different optional condition. The comparison is descriptive; there is no mixed-model ranked claim. Shareable replays omit private notes and never include other applications or personal memory.

**Memorable event.** Your companion uses the empty case from an earlier retrieval as ballast to hold an escape gate. You recognize the reuse lesson, but must detach the right tether and cross while it remains anchored. The surprise is a legal physical combination you did not explicitly request. The clip shows your earlier teaching, the changed circumstance, and both participants' actions.

## Agent behavior contract

| Question | Version 1.0 decision |
| --- | --- |
| What does it observe? | Local visible objects and their known properties, recent action outcomes, current mission restrictions, shared player markings, relevant lesson examples, its equipment, and known reachable routes |
| What is hidden? | Unexplored site information, unrevealed optional objectives, future events, player application data, and authored solution metadata |
| What can it do? | Inspect, move to a legal location, carry/place permitted objects, anchor, connect allowed equipment, hold/release a mechanism, propose a joint sequence, ask a focused question, or wait |
| How often does it reason? | Event-driven at planning boundaries, changed constraints, and explicit teaching; target average 24 model calls per play hour, excluding an explicitly measured retry allowance |
| How often does it act? | Ordinary execution follows a validated multi-action plan continuously; action speed does not require a model call per frame or footstep |
| Constraints | Finite legal action vocabulary; bounded plan length and inputs; no arbitrary code, web access, messages, purchases, or access to the host computer |
| Memory | A reversible game-specific lesson library and short event summaries; retrieve relevant records rather than sending the entire history |
| Communication | Pointing, action previews, short contextual questions, and optional typed clarification; no mandatory open-ended chat |
| Can it fail? | It may choose a legal but suboptimal plan or ask for clarification. Invalid outputs and service failures are product faults, not earned gameplay penalties |
| Can the player replace it? | Copy or reset a lesson profile while retaining the companion's appearance and collection; no paid replacement or hidden intelligence roll |
| Model differences | Official behavior version is recorded on challenges/replays; model changes require regression evaluation, notice, and retained profile snapshots |
| External agents | Not supported for controlling play in 1.0. Game-specific lesson export/import is supported as inspectable data, not executable instructions |

A legal-action validator determines whether a proposed action exists and is permitted. Deterministic simulation determines physics, resource consumption, mission conditions, and success. Safe execution can refuse an invalid action and request a new plan. It must not secretly turn a bad high-level plan into a winning one and then attribute that success to learning.

Latency target: ordinary movement responds immediately; planned interpretation should usually complete within a few seconds while the player can inspect the scene. Consequential simulation pauses when the player is waiting on required agent reasoning. At eight seconds without a result, show a clear delayed-response state; at fifteen seconds, offer retry or checkpoint save/exit. A brief local fallback can hold position or complete an already valid action. It cannot masquerade as the full adaptive experience during an extended outage.

Online service is required for new adaptive decisions. The store must say so. Local saves, previously recorded replays, and the practice viewer remain accessible during outages. Service continuity, an end-of-service plan, and support funding are business obligations; a speculative future local model is not a promised offline mode.

## Fairness, safety, and support

This is cooperative single-player play with descriptive challenge sharing. Buying a stronger model is not part of the game. There is no economy, tradable agent, prize ladder, or monetary scoring. Imported profiles are validated game records with size limits; arbitrary instructions in names or notes are treated as data.

Shared replays are opt-in and stripped of private free text by default. Names and permitted short public captions are filtered and reportable. A recipient can block further sharing. There is no public chat or generated inter-player messaging. The companion should never solicit real-world secrets, claim emotional distress when the player leaves, or issue instructions outside the game.

Provider processing is explained at onboarding. Keep only the game context necessary for behavior; do not send microphone recordings because voice is absent. Research/training reuse of play data requires a separate opt-in. Proposed retention: ordinary diagnostic events for 30 days, account-linked service metrics for 90 days, and saved profiles/replays until deleted by the player. A reported replay may be retained for the case with that exception explained. These are product policies to be checked against launch jurisdictions, not legal conclusions.

Support distinguishes a bad lesson, a legal risky choice, an execution bug, a model-policy refusal, and a network outage. The player can attach a privacy-reviewed event bundle and profile version. Offer one-click restoration of the last sound profile/checkpoint. Never ban a player solely because the model generated an inappropriate response. Support goals: acknowledge ordinary tickets within two business days and offer clear incident status during service disruption.

## Economics and monetization

**Proposed offer:** $24.99 US list price, regional pricing, complete campaign and challenge mode, included core adaptive service. A free two-site demonstration uses the same actual interaction model. No energy, credits, paid intelligence, loot boxes, or battle pass. Optional later expansions must add substantial sites and mechanisms; cosmetics are not required to rescue base-game economics.

The operating target is **24 planning calls per play hour**, each averaging 2,000 input and 400 billed output tokens, plus 20% retry/overhead. At the mid price reference in the shared evaluation this costs $0.04608 per hour: about $0.46 for ten hours, $1.84 for forty, and $5.53 for 120. This is a design budget to test, not achieved performance. A maximum session call rate of 60/hour would cost $0.1152/hour at the same assumptions. Ordinary play should not encounter a usage meter or forced pause to satisfy the target.

Model a blended lifetime service reserve of **$4 per sold unit** only if observed use supports it. At $18 realized transaction revenue before refunds/platform share, 8% refunds, and a 30% platform allowance, contribution is $7.592 before fixed costs and acquisition. A $1.35 million illustrative total budget requires about 178,000 units at this contribution. If capability requires the high reference rate, forty hours at the target call count costs about $7.60 in inference alone and this offer needs revision. [Shared assumptions and rates](/Users/safa.safakhou/repos/mine/random_idea/product-discovery/03-evaluation.md).

Do not greenlight production based on the reserve. Measure cohort playtime, inference, refunds, concurrency peaks, and support burden. Budget a 24-month service commitment after sale for capacity planning, while explicitly modeling longer use; this is not a plan to terminate access at month 24. The economic downside is a successful long-play game with insufficient revenue per player.

## Launch and company plan

Initial players are puzzle/adventure and systemic-game enthusiasts, including those indifferent to AI. Show a ten-second sequence of demonstration, a changed situation, and the companion applying the idea. The trailer's longer section must show the human moving, manipulating, and recovering; a reel of autonomous antics would misrepresent the game.

Recruit relevant creators who explain mechanisms and tell emergent stories. Build an opt-in community around sharing approaches, avoid presenting unexpected behavior as sentience, and publish clear behavior-change notes. No large simultaneous player population is required: one paying player receives the full game. Sharing provides word-of-mouth, not a dependency on a network effect.

Launch-day requirements include all 24 sites and variants, ending, controller/accessibility coverage, demonstration, accurate AI/online disclosures, support/status pages, English UI and tested generated text, profile recovery, and tested peak capacity. Additional language promises require separate behavioral testing and are outside the base 1.0 scope. Do not list untested language support on the store.

Milestones are evidence-based rather than calendar commitments: select and test the teaching interaction; establish the site production unit and cost; complete representative region content; produce the full campaign; run repeated external play and reliability tests; then set a public launch date. An internal prototype can evaluate risk during later implementation planning, but it is not the paid release described here.

Partnership opportunities include game publishers with puzzle/companion experience and inference vendors offering contracted capacity. No partner or discount is assumed. First expansion direction: a new region introducing two mechanics that recombine with old lessons. Live co-op comes only after proving that human ownership of lessons remains clear; a creator platform is not the default roadmap.

## Product analytics and release criteria

The primary product signal is a **completed expedition containing an identifiable useful human intervention and a useful adaptive agent action, followed by a voluntary return for another distinct challenge**. This is a measurement definition, not a result. Do not optimize raw agent turns, conversation length, or time spent correcting failures.

Track first mutual payoff, tutorial assistance, examples marked, comparisons rejected, lessons applied in changed situations, overrides, unnecessary questions, checkpoints restored, voluntary retries, site completion, optional objectives, repeat play, share use, refunds, and support categories. Service metrics cover billed tokens, costs, latency percentiles, invalid actions, outages, and concurrency. Behavioral telemetry should minimize raw free text.

Proposed release gates: at least 80% of representative first-time players understand and reach the first mutual payoff within ten minutes without facilitator rescue; at least 70% can identify their contribution after a site; teaching provides a clear qualitative advantage over competent presets across repeated sessions; no known progress-corrupting issues; all tested illegal or unauthorized actions rejected; at least 99.9% successful routine execution of valid committed actions; successful checkpoint restoration in all tested interruption cases; and measured cohort costs fit the reserve with a stress-tested tail. Percentages are proposed decision thresholds, not industry benchmarks or achieved statistics.

## Risks, open questions, and long-term vision

The largest risk is that teaching is either unnecessary or tedious. Next are brittle transfer, unclear attribution, content that exposes only superficial variation, and an inference tail that exceeds the purchase price. The key unanswered question is whether players value a particular learned approach enough to return when a preset would be simpler.

Reject or redesign if teaching loses to presets, if mistakes are mostly software faults, if useful transfer demands unbounded language, or if paid-included use cannot be supported economically. A stronger model should improve the partnership without making the human's game disappear.

Long-term, Fieldwise could become a series of adventures centered on persistent player-shaped companions and transferable game-specific methods. The vision is a body of shared experience expressed through play. Cross-game portability is optional and must preserve meaning; exporting a text profile alone does not establish that an identity survives.

## Founder and investor pitch

Fieldwise turns teaching into an adventure mechanic. You explore crafted field sites with a capable companion, show it the approaches you value, and then see those approaches adapted to unfamiliar problems. You still move, manipulate tools, set priorities, and carry out the human half of joint maneuvers. The memorable moment is not that the companion speaks; it is recognizing your influence in a solution you did not explicitly script.

The first product is a $24.99 PC game with 24 complete expeditions, challenge variants, a reversible lesson history, and private sharing. It serves puzzle/adventure players immediately without an AI account, a public population, or a creator economy. Its distinction rests on reliable learning interactions and authored systems that reward transfer. Those capabilities must be proven against competent conventional baselines.

The commercial case is conditional: production investment should follow evidence that teaching is enjoyable and that event-based inference fits lifetime unit economics. The illustrative budget recovery is roughly 178,000 units, not a sales forecast. Funding would pay for game and companion interaction design, content, engineering, art/audio, accessibility, QA, and launch/service reserves; no funding amount or delivery date is committed before technical planning. The potential company is a focused game studio with a distinctive companion relationship, expandable through new adventures rather than a speculative agent platform.
