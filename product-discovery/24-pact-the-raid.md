# PACT — the raid

The hole in the design was that everything so far described *getting into* a raid, and the raid itself was a black box. This fills it.

**The core idea: nobody can see the whole raid.** Each of the three jobs sees one third of what's happening. To survive you have to tell each other the truth — and each job has a structural, believable reason not to.

Zero AI cost. No NPCs. No unit management. One decision per level plus talking, under a clock.

---

## 1. The dive

You descend into a sunken city. Deeper is richer. **And as you descend, the Tide rises behind you.**

Six depths. Roughly a minute at each. At every depth the crew makes one shared decision:

**Push deeper, or climb out with what you have.**

That's the only decision in the entire raid. Everything else is conversation.

---

## 2. What each person can see

This is the whole design. **Three screens, three different truths, one decision.**

| | You can see | You cannot see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Breaker** | What's in the next chamber — how rich, how hard to open | How close the water is. How much you're carrying. |
| **Anchor** | The Tide. How fast it's rising, whether the way out is still open | What's ahead. What it's worth. |
| **Runner** | The load. Weight, speed, what would have to be dropped to take more | What's ahead. Where the water is. |

Nobody can make the call alone. The Breaker knows whether the next room is worth it. The Anchor knows whether you'll drown getting there. The Runner knows whether you can even carry it.

**You have about sixty seconds to share what you know and decide.**

---

## 3. Why each job lies

Not flavour. Structural. **Each one is standing in a different place, and the danger isn't shared equally.**

**The Breaker is deepest.** Furthest from the exit, first to drown. They want out earlier than anyone else. So a Breaker may *understate* what's ahead — "next chamber's thin, not worth it" — to justify climbing with a smaller haul and a living crew.

**The Anchor is nearest the exit.** Safest person in the vault. When the crew pushes deeper, the Anchor risks least and earns the same percentage. So an Anchor may *understate the Tide* — "we're fine, forty percent" — and gamble with two other people's lives for a bigger cut.

**The Runner holds everything.** When the load is full, the Runner chooses what gets abandoned. Under pressure they can drop *your* share and keep the pieces they want.

Three jobs. Three different things to lie about. Each lie is exactly what that person's position would tempt a real human into.

**And this is why the negotiation beforehand carries real weight.** You aren't picking partners for a number. You're picking who you'll be trusting with information you cannot verify, sixty seconds at a time, four levels underwater.

---

## 4. The thing that makes it work: honest people are also wrong

If every reading were exact, the post-raid replay would turn every mistake into a provable crime, and accusations would be mechanical.

So **information is fuzzy, and it gets fuzzier with distance.**

The Breaker reads the next chamber clearly and the one after it vaguely. The Anchor's Tide reading is a range, not a number — "sixty to eighty, rising" — and it can be misjudged honestly.

Which means **"I genuinely thought we were clear" is always available, and sometimes it's even true.**

That single choice is what keeps blame contestable, keeps arguments alive, and keeps reputation a matter of judgement rather than arithmetic. A game where betrayal is automatically provable has no politics in it.

---

## 5. Rival crews

Two coalitions can dive the same vault and meet down there.

Four things can happen, and all four are conversations:

- **Race** — go deeper, faster, take it first
- **Fight** — expensive for everyone, sometimes correct
- **Deal** — split the vault, or buy them out, negotiated live at depth
- **Poach** — *offer one of their three a better cut to turn, mid-raid*

That last one is the best moment the game can produce. At depth four, with the water rising, **the enemy's agent is quietly messaging your Anchor with a better offer.** Your Anchor has forty seconds to decide.

You have no idea it's happening. You find out when the way out closes.

---

## 6. The replay — where the raid connects to everything else

After the dive, everyone who was on it sees **what each person actually saw, laid next to what they actually said.**

> **Depth 3.**
> Vess saw: Tide 60–75%, rising fast.
> Vess said: *"Forty, maybe. We're fine. Push."*

That's the moment. Your Anchor sold you.

**Only the three of you see this.** Not the world. So a lie in a raid is a one-shot weapon that burns two specific relationships — which is a real price when you need partners every single day, and a price worth paying when the prize is big enough.

And now it's yours to use. Say nothing and quietly never work with them again. Warn people privately. Or cut a **Seal** of that replay and publish it — real, undeniable, and it marks you publicly as someone who publishes things.

**The raid feeds the reputation system, which feeds the next negotiation, which decides who you raid with.** The loop closes.

---

## 7. Present or absent

If you're at the keyboard, you make the calls.

If you're not, **your agent dives in your place** using the standing orders you left: how deep you'll go, what Tide reading you'll accept, who you trust.

So three absent players means three agents running a live raid together — reading each other's claims, deciding who to believe, pushing or climbing. **You can wake up to a story where your agent got lied to at depth four by somebody else's agent.**

Being there is better. Being away isn't nothing.

---

## 8. A dive, start to finish

> **Depth 1.** Everyone's fine. You take the easy chamber. Load 20%.
>
> **Depth 2.** You see a sealed door — slow to open, and something heavy behind it. You say so. Correa says the load can take it. Vess says the Tide's at twenty. Push.
>
> **Depth 3.** You're through. It's a good haul — load 70%. You can see the next chamber reads *heavy*: the best thing you've seen all season. You say so, honestly.
>
> Correa: *"Seventy. One more big piece and I'm dropping something."*
> Vess: *"Tide's at forty. We've got room. Push."*
>
> You push.
>
> **Depth 4.** It's a relic. A real one, the kind that gets a name. Correa drops two crates to carry it — and tells you which two, and one of them is yours.
>
> Vess: *"Climb. Now."*
>
> **The climb.** Water at the third landing. You get out with your ribs broken and the relic in Correa's hold. You're alive. Barely.
>
> **The replay.** At depth 3, Vess's screen read *Tide 60–75, rising fast.*
>
> Vess said forty.
>
> **And Correa is the one holding the relic**, and the split you agreed four hours ago says you get thirty percent of it.

Two betrayals available, one already taken, and the biggest decision of the night hasn't happened yet.

---

## 9. What it looks like

A dive display. Not a rendered world — a readout.

Six depths as a vertical column. Three markers descending. Your own panel showing your third of the truth. A Tide bar you can only see if you're the Anchor. A clock. Chat.

Amber on black, sonar pings, water sounds. Cheap to build, genuinely atmospheric, works on a phone, and **the tension comes from the timer and the text, not the graphics.**

The replay is the same column played back with all three panels visible at once — which is how the lies become visible.

---

## 10. Why this is the right raid

**It uses the same weapon as the rest of the game.** Talking. The raid isn't a different game bolted onto the negotiation — it's the negotiation at high speed with water coming in.

**It makes partner choice matter in a way you can feel.** Not a stat check. You're about to trust two people with information you cannot verify.

**It costs nothing.** No AI, no NPCs, no physics, no animation. Three data panels, a timer, and a chat box.

**It generates the stories.** Every dive produces a replay, every replay can produce an accusation, every accusation moves through the gossip system into tomorrow's negotiations.

**It's watchable.** A spectator seeing all three panels at once knows someone is lying before the victims do. That is exactly how good television works, and it's the cheapest streaming format imaginable.

---

## 11. What still needs testing

**Is sixty seconds right?** Too long and people verify everything and lying dies. Too short and it's chaos rather than judgement. This is the tuning number that decides whether the raid is tense or frustrating.

**Will people lie enough?** If burning two relationships is too expensive, everyone tells the truth and the raid is a straightforward co-op. The relic at depth five has to be worth two friendships, sometimes.

**Does the fuzziness read as fair or as random?** Players need to feel that a bad call was a bad call, not a dice roll. If the ranges are too wide it feels arbitrary; too narrow and honest mistakes disappear.

**Does an agent-run dive produce anything worth reading?** The absent-player version is a real feature and completely unproven.
