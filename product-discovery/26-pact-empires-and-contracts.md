# PACT — no map, empires, and contracts

Three changes to [PACT v2](/Users/safa.safakhou/repos/mine/random_idea/product-discovery/25-pact-v2.md). They fit together better than they look, and one of them solves a problem the earlier rounds couldn't.

---

## 1. There is no map

No coordinates. No travel time. No scrolling around looking for someone to hit. Your kingdom exists; so does everyone else's.

**Removing the map makes the most important mechanic in the game stronger, not weaker.**

In Clash of Clans you find targets by scrolling until you see a weak one. Here there is nothing to scroll. So how do you find anyone at all?

- Somebody tells you
- You buy the information
- You already know them, because you've dealt with them
- The public Record shows they did something loud

**Without a map, information is the only way to find a target.** The intelligence market stops being a nice extra layer and becomes the thing the whole game runs on. A player with no contacts is a player who literally cannot find anyone to attack.

That's the strongest possible version of the mechanic, and it comes from deleting something rather than building something.

---

## 2. Allegiance replaces geography

The setting is the age of empires. Kingdoms, banners, courts, wax seals, tribute.

**Where you are is who you serve.**

You are independent, or you are sworn to an empire. That is your position in the world, it's public, and it's the first thing anyone learns about you.

### Why anyone pays tribute

A vassal sends a share of production upward, automatically. In return the overlord provides things a kingdom cannot get alone:

**Protection.** Hitting a vassal means answering to their overlord. That is not a rule — it's a threat, and it's worth exactly as much as the overlord's willingness to act on it.

**Shared intelligence.** The empire pools what its members know. Given that information is how you find anyone, this is enormous. An independent kingdom is blind by comparison.

**Supply security.** The empire guarantees its members' grain.

### Why anyone becomes an overlord

Tribute income, and a great deal of it.

**But an empire is only as strong as its reputation for actually defending people.** Fail to answer when a vassal is attacked and word gets around. Vassals leave. Tribute dries up. Rivals start picking off your weakest members deliberately, just to show you can't protect them.

That's self-regulating, it needs no rules, and it produces the best drama in the game:

- A vassal quietly negotiating with a rival empire while still paying you
- Several vassals agreeing to stop paying on the same day
- An overlord letting one vassal burn because defending them isn't worth the cost — and everyone watching to see whether he does

## 3. Contracts

This is the biggest addition, and you're right that everything being verbal is unsatisfying.

A contract is a standing agreement the world executes automatically.

> *100 iron per hour, for three days.*
>
> *10 grain per hour from me, 5 silver per hour from you, ongoing, seven days' notice to end.*
>
> *500 spearmen lent for two days, returned at the end.*
>
> *2,000 iron now, 2,400 repaid over twenty hours.*

Once both sides sign, it just runs. You don't have to be online. You don't have to do anything.

### What this fixes

**Your agent's work now lasts.** Before, a negotiation produced one transaction. Now a good negotiation produces something that keeps paying you for a week. An hour of your agent's time can set up an income that survives you being away — which makes the political game feel like it's building toward something instead of resetting daily.

**The contract network is the map.** You wanted no geography. Fine — but a world needs structure, and this is better structure than coordinates. Who supplies whom. Who owes whom. Who is quietly dependent on a rival. That web is the real shape of the world, it's partly public and partly secret, and it's worth paying to learn.

**New players get a way in.** More on this below, and it's the part I'm most pleased with.

### Breaking a contract

**You can always break one. Instantly. Nothing stops you.** That's consistent with everything else in this game.

What matters is *how*.

| | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **Expiry** | It ends. Nothing happens. Clean. |
| **Notice** | You give the agreed warning and walk away. Slightly awkward, no lasting harm. |
| **Default** | You cut it off with no warning. **Permanent public record.** |

So there's a real difference between ending a deal properly and cutting someone off. Both are always available.

**And cutting someone off at the exact moment they need it is the most devastating move in the game.** Your grain supplier defaults the hour your army marches. Your creditor calls in a loan while you're under siege. Nothing in the rules prevents it. Everyone will know. Sometimes it's still worth it.

### Bonds — and the thing they fix

When you sign, you can post a **bond**: resources held by the world, forfeited to the other side if you default.

This lets you **buy credibility you haven't earned yet.**

That solves the cold-start problem I flagged earlier and had no good answer for. A new player has no reputation and nobody will deal with them. But they can post a bond. They're saying: *I can't prove I'm good for it, so here's my money — take it if I'm not.*

It's exactly how trust actually got built between strangers before institutions existed, it's period-correct for the setting, and it gives a newcomer a real path into the political economy on day one.

It also creates its own dilemma. Demand a bond from an established player and you've publicly said you don't trust them. That's an insult with consequences.

### Debt

Lending with interest deserves calling out separately, because it does something the rest of the system doesn't.

**Debt creates a relationship that outlives the deal.** A creditor has an ongoing interest in the debtor staying alive and solvent — which means creditors defend their debtors, and that's a reason to protect someone you don't even like.

It also gives a weak player a route to growth that isn't just waiting, and it makes default a genuine public event with several angry parties.

### Keep it to a few knobs

The danger with contracts is that they become a spreadsheet. **The whole thing must fit on a phone screen:** what flows each way, how often, how long, how much notice, and whether there's a bond.

Five fields. If it needs a sixth, don't add it.

## 4. What this changes elsewhere

**Attacking.** You can no longer browse for victims. You attack people you've learned about, which means the intelligence market gates all aggression. Combat itself is unchanged — automatic, dice, expensive.

**Offline.** This gets better without weakening what you asked for. Contracts keep running while you're away, so you don't come back to ruins. But **no new deals get made without you**, and contracts expire, and someone who was online took the opportunity you missed. Presence still wins; absence just isn't catastrophic.

**Multi-accounting.** Contracts are transfers, so every safeguard still applies — build time as the real bottleneck, storage caps, the trade-balance tax, no surplus at small sizes. **Watch loans especially:** a loan to an alt that is never repaid is just a disguised transfer. The balance tax should treat unpaid debt as the transfer it actually is.

**The Seal.** Now perfect for the setting. A sealed excerpt of a conversation is a wax seal on a document — real, verifiable, and you still choose what it says by choosing where to cut.

---

## 5. What to watch

**Contracts could make the world too stable.** If everyone locks into comfortable supply deals, the politics calcify and nothing happens. Expiry is the pressure valve: **every contract ends, and every ending is a renegotiation.** Keeping durations short enough that the world stays in motion is a real tuning problem.

**Empires could become permanent.** The strongest empire takes the best vassals, gets stronger, takes more. The counters are real but unproven: tribute makes the overlord a target worth toppling, vassals can coordinate a revolt, and the biggest empire is the one everybody else has a reason to gang up on.

**Tribute needs to feel worth paying.** If protection and shared intelligence aren't genuinely valuable, vassalage reads as a tax with no benefit and everyone goes independent. This is the number to watch in the first test.

**Five fields, not fifteen.** The pull toward a contract-editing spreadsheet will be constant. Resist it.
