# Eighteen agent game concepts

All names are working directions, not cleared brands. All gameplay descriptions are original design hypotheses. Commercial potential describes a plausible audience and business fit, not measured demand. The concepts span teaching, direct cooperation, construction, deception, performance, management, spectatorship, and creation; differences are in the repeated human activity, not just the setting.

## 1. Apprentice Expedition

**Core idea and fantasy:** Develop a capable companion by showing it how to approach problems, then undertake increasingly unfamiliar expeditions together. Feel the pride of having taught something that can surprise you.

**Human and agent roles:** The human demonstrates approaches, changes the environment, handles their own equipment, and chooses commitments. The agent observes examples, infers reusable lessons, proposes approaches, and performs complementary work.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** In a compact obstacle space, show how to stabilize a load, test the companion's interpretation, and attempt a crossing together. A later situation changes materials or geometry. The pleasure is seeing transfer, rescuing a flawed plan, and discovering a better shared method. Play need not use a chat box.

**Why agents matter:** Transfer from examples and contextual adaptation are the principal mechanics; a list of fixed tricks would substantially narrow the game.

**Mastery and retention:** Choosing informative examples, understanding generalization, coordinating actions, and deciding which habits to keep. New expedition conditions test familiar skills. Attachment rests on shared history and chosen lessons.

**Social and commercial potential:** Share short lesson-to-result clips and optional private challenge visits. Fits a premium puzzle/adventure audience with expansion content.

**Weakness and failure:** Teaching may feel like troubleshooting. Kill if preset behavior produces comparable ownership or if players must repeatedly correct reliable basic tasks.

## 2. Relay

**Core idea and fantasy:** You and an autonomous partner explore connected spaces with different information and tools. Be one half of a resourceful partnership.

**Human and agent roles:** The human directly explores one side, selects evidence to communicate, manipulates mechanisms, and commits to timings. The agent investigates its side and adapts a shared plan. Neither has a complete global view.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Your partner finds that a power relay will flood your corridor. You investigate a bypass while it holds a temporary route open. You exchange observations and revise the plan when a door jams. Tension comes from commitments and incomplete understanding, with accessible pause options.

**Why agents matter:** Open coordination and independent initiative across changing circumstances exceed a canned hint companion. That difference must actually be demonstrated.

**Mastery and retention:** Efficient communication, anticipation, evidence triage, timing, and recovery. New arrangements of connected systems create repeat adventures.

**Social and commercial potential:** Easily explained cooperative adventure; compare solutions and optional two-human/two-agent missions. A premium campaign is plausible without public matchmaking.

**Weakness and failure:** Could collapse into a conventional puzzle game with a talking guide. Kill if communication is mostly instruction following, or if a competent partner solves everything worth thinking about.

## 3. House Rules

**Core idea and fantasy:** Build situations that an adaptive agent must interpret, then send your own agent into other people's creations. Be a designer of clever, readable challenges.

**Human and agent roles:** The human constructs environments and contextual rules, predicts behavior, and coaches between attempts. Agents explore and assemble plans from available actions.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Build a museum delivery challenge using doors, signs, delicate cargo, and transport devices. Watch the visitor use an arrangement you did not anticipate; revise without making the task impossible. Compete on elegant solutions or a constrained design brief, not merely failure rate.

**Why agents matter:** The visitor can interpret new combinations and instructions, instead of replaying a known path or executing the designer's script.

**Mastery and retention:** Anticipating interpretations, designing fair constraints, and transferring lessons across creators' worlds. Retention grows from challenge exchange and a recognizable design style.

**Social and commercial potential:** Strong creator ecosystem hypothesis and asynchronous exchange. Sell a complete construction game with authored challenges before relying on a public catalogue.

**Weakness and failure:** May become a prompt-injection contest, benchmark, or specialist editor. Kill if entertainment depends on model bugs or most players only watch creators.

## 4. Curtain Call

**Core idea and fantasy:** Perform improvised scenes with an agent cast while physically staging their consequences. Feel like a quick-thinking performer and director.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans introduce actions, props, and reversals; agents maintain motives, remember scene facts, and react through dialogue and stage actions.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** A scene must end with a stolen crown returned without its owner discovering the theft. You switch a prop while your partner invents a distracting ceremony. The human adapts to the new premise and sets up its payoff. Success uses observable story conditions; comic taste is left to people.

**Why agents matter:** Sustained, contextual improvisation provides most of the possibility space.

**Mastery and retention:** Making useful offers, incorporating callbacks, and recovering coherently. Return for new briefs, casts, and human groups.

**Social and commercial potential:** Excellent clip potential and a party audience; premium scene packs or group access could fit.

**Weakness and failure:** Quality may depend on already being good at improv; actors can dominate. Kill if ordinary players mostly read dialogue, if judging feels arbitrary, or if jokes become interchangeable.

## 5. Alibi

**Core idea and fantasy:** Human–agent pairs conduct social deduction while each keeps private knowledge and obligations. Feel clever for identifying the exact contradiction that exposes a bluff.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans choose which evidence to disclose and who to trust. Agents investigate, interview, and make claims within their information limits. Both may have in-fiction reasons to conceal facts.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Commit a shared alibi before a hearing, compare witness reports, then use limited questions to expose discrepancies. Other humans can read social intent while agents handle more detailed evidence tracking.

**Why agents matter:** Each team has a semi-independent participant whose statements and investigation create additional consequences.

**Mastery and retention:** Information discipline, interrogation choices, and reasoning about incentives; refreshed cases and opponent behavior drive rematches.

**Social and commercial potential:** Strong small-group game-night pitch; private lobbies could avoid public matchmaking at first.

**Weakness and failure:** Agents may leak roles, overwhelm conversation, or remove human deduction. Requires significant moderation and clear rules. Kill if prompt attacks outperform in-world inference.

## 6. Split Verdict

**Core idea and fantasy:** Build a case with an agent advocate, then participate in adversarial hearings. Be the person who finds and times the decisive evidence.

**Human and agent roles:** Human investigators establish facts and choose stakes; agent advocates question witnesses and construct arguments from permitted evidence.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Discover a timestamp discrepancy, reserve it while a witness commits to a version of events, then interrupt at a permitted moment. Judgments depend on established facts and explicit procedural rules rather than an unconstrained model's opinion.

**Why agents matter:** Adaptive cross-examination can produce responses to approaches beyond fixed dialogue trees.

**Mastery and retention:** Building coherent cases, anticipating counterarguments, and choosing disclosure timing. New authored cases and alternate arguments supply replay.

**Social and commercial potential:** Share dramatic reveals; a premium mystery audience is plausible.

**Weakness and failure:** Evidence can become busywork while the agent performs the satisfying reasoning. Case production is expensive. Kill if the best action is always “ask the advocate what to do.”

## 7. Living Blueprint

**Core idea and fantasy:** Breed and shape an adaptive organism by altering its habitat and reinforcement. Feel like a field naturalist discovering how life responds.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans build ecological conditions and choose developmental interventions; organisms learn foraging, shelter, and cooperative behavior.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Rear a creature in a windy habitat, then change the food distribution and observe the consequences. Rescue a habitat by adjusting shelter and resources instead of issuing commands.

**Why agents matter:** Adaptation is the subject of play; without it, this is a static ecosystem puzzle.

**Mastery and retention:** Experimental design, causal inference, and prediction over generations. Retain lineages and compare different solutions.

**Social and commercial potential:** Creature sharing and community discoveries; premium simulation audience, with a smaller accessible segment than the visual fantasy suggests.

**Weakness and failure:** Slow feedback, opaque learning, and complexity can make observation dull. Training is technically difficult. Kill if apparent diversity is cosmetic or players cannot explain useful interventions.

## 8. Offstage League

**Core idea and fantasy:** Fans and their agents shape a fictional competition through limited interventions. Be part of a community creating legends around an autonomous spectacle.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans back teams and vote on bounded rule changes; agents compete and form in-game affiliations.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Before a short event, choose a roster or environmental modifier; watch a consequential highlight; afterward bargain with other fans over the next rule amendment.

**Why agents matter:** Adaptive entrants respond to changing rules and carry behavior across events.

**Mastery and retention:** Anticipation, collective coordination, and interpreting a changing league. Shared rituals and history create return visits.

**Social and commercial potential:** Strong community narrative potential; memberships or cosmetic support could fit if an audience already exists.

**Weakness and failure:** Individual agency is weak and the product needs an audience and continuing editorial work. Kill as a first product if it needs a large simultaneous crowd to be enjoyable. Spectatorship itself is not disqualifying.

## 9. Second Self

**Core idea and fantasy:** Train an evolving rival that learns your habits and exposes them in tightly scoped contests. Experience measurable self-mastery.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans play directly and select practice priorities; the agent models recurring habits, adapts its strategy, and becomes a personalized opponent.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Win a short pursuit duel, inspect the habit the rival exploited, practice an alternative, and rematch under a changed layout.

**Why agents matter:** The personally adapting opponent is the progression system.

**Mastery and retention:** Improving execution and breaking predictable habits; satisfying if rematches reveal real growth.

**Social and commercial potential:** Share rivals or challenge friends' training partners. Premium competitive training game, or a feature licensed into existing titles.

**Weakness and failure:** Training may serve a more compelling underlying sport rather than justify a new game. Kill if a few scripted difficulty levels feel equivalent or if adaptation feels like unfair counterpicking.

## 10. Treaty Table

**Core idea and fantasy:** Negotiate agreements through agents whose authority you can shape, while personally deciding which concessions to make. Feel like an inventive dealmaker rather than an optimizer of a single score.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans set private priorities and authorize binding commitments. Agents explore offers, identify exchanges, and maintain conditional agreements. Neither can invent resources or change enforcement rules.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** In a 25-minute negotiation, several islands need different combinations of transport, water, and power. Your envoy discovers a three-party exchange, but it requires you to abandon an optional objective. You revise its mandate and choose whether to reveal your scarcity. The negotiation closes with consequences everyone can inspect.

**Why agents matter:** Agents can discover and explain conditional exchanges across heterogeneous priorities, making a richer bargaining space manageable.

**Mastery and retention:** Mandate design, valuation, disclosure, and recognizing coalition opportunities. Opponents and new resource arrangements support repeated play.

**Social and commercial potential:** Private game-night groups and negotiation enthusiasts; a small premium audience is plausible.

**Weakness and failure:** The enjoyable conversation may happen between machines. Stronger models and collusion threaten competition. Kill if humans spend most of the session approving obviously beneficial offers.

## 11. Night Shift

**Core idea and fantasy:** Run a small service operation with a crew that can adapt to unusual requests while you handle arrivals and emergencies. Feel like the person holding a capable but imperfect team together.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans triage work, arrange the workspace, and act directly at a service station. Agents coordinate preparation, substitutions, and work handoffs within clear limits.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** A repair depot receives a rush of mismatched equipment. One agent proposes cannibalizing an unfinished order for a critical delivery; you decide, rearrange a workbench, and finish the customer-facing job while the crew adapts.

**Why agents matter:** Contextual substitution and coordination could produce solutions beyond fixed recipes. That must be substantive, not dialogue layered over ordinary workers.

**Mastery and retention:** Workspace design, prioritization, reading bottlenecks, and balancing reliability against improvisation. New tools and request combinations drive return visits.

**Social and commercial potential:** Familiar management pitch with optional co-op and shareable recovery moments. Premium service simulation is plausible.

**Weakness and failure:** Conventional scheduling may be sufficient, and language-model mistakes can feel like broken pathfinding. Reject unless adaptive work produces a distinct human skill loop.

## 12. Counterfeit Club

**Core idea and fantasy:** Create decoys for another team's agent while helping your own agent distinguish genuine objects from imitations. Feel like an ingenious trickster and perceptive investigator.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans arrange visual and contextual evidence. Agents inspect, ask bounded questions, and classify. Humans decide when to spend scarce physical tests that expose a decoy.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Design a fake delivery using packaging, routing clues, and a forged in-fiction story; then inspect another team's shipment. A weight test is reliable but consumes time. Watching an opponent notice the one clue you concealed poorly creates the reveal.

**Why agents matter:** The decoy targets an adaptive interpretation rather than a fixed recognition rule.

**Mastery and retention:** Building internally consistent deceptions and choosing informative tests. Fresh human-made artifacts drive rematches.

**Social and commercial potential:** Short private matches and strong reveal clips; a party-game price is plausible.

**Weakness and failure:** Can degrade into exploiting model blind spots and out-of-fiction instructions. Expensive visual inference may dominate costs. Reject if model quirks are more important than readable in-world evidence.

## 13. Chorus

**Core idea and fantasy:** Perform a musical pattern with adaptive partners that respond to your musical intent. Feel like a member of an ensemble that can understand and develop your ideas.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans play notes, place rhythmic motifs, and signal transitions. Agents handle complementary parts, anticipate changes, and improvise within a shared harmonic vocabulary.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Introduce a syncopated phrase; a partner answers; sustain the exchange while navigating musical objectives such as moving between moods without breaking the pulse. The human must perform the transitions, not merely request a song.

**Why agents matter:** Responsive musical interpretation and adaptation are the activity, rather than an automatically generated soundtrack.

**Mastery and retention:** Timing, listening, motif development, and signaling. New instruments and arrangement challenges support expressive replay.

**Social and commercial potential:** Collaborative performances and recordings; premium creative game or instrument-like product.

**Weakness and failure:** Very tight latency constraints, music rights, accessibility to nonmusicians, and an unclear boundary between instrument and game. Park unless the team has relevant music-game expertise.

## 14. Away Team

**Core idea and fantasy:** Send an agent on an expedition and respond to a few consequential discoveries each day. Feel like the sponsor of an unfolding adventure that fits around life.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans provision expeditions, choose priorities, and resolve consequential forks. The agent navigates between those decisions and brings back evidence and discoveries.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Examine a short report showing a blocked pass and limited food. Change the expedition objective or authorize a risky detour; later inspect what happened and choose the next journey. Important irreversible choices wait for the player.

**Why agents matter:** Adaptive responses to unusual combinations can make reports and outcomes specific to the player's instructions.

**Mastery and retention:** Preparation, risk judgment, and interpreting incomplete reports. Anticipation and accumulated expedition history drive return visits.

**Social and commercial potential:** Share discoveries and assist friends asynchronously; potentially suitable for mobile.

**Weakness and failure:** Human play may amount to notifications and a few buttons, while inference runs for absent players. Reject if a branching adventure delivers the same value more clearly or if return habits depend on fear of missing out.

## 15. Know Me

**Core idea and fantasy:** Coach an agent to understand your tastes, then play a guessing game with friends about what it will choose. Feel recognized, and discover funny differences between how you see yourself and how others see you.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans choose examples and predict one another's agent responses. Agents generalize preferences from game-specific evidence, without needing personal-account memory.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Select a handful of fictional holiday plans, objects, or outfits. Friends make offers intended to delight your agent; you reveal your own choice and compare. People earn points for accurately predicting the human, not manipulating a model judge.

**Why agents matter:** A portable, contestable model of a player's taste creates the prediction target and feedback loop.

**Mastery and retention:** Giving informative examples and reading people. Repeat value comes from new groups and themes, with limited likely solo depth.

**Social and commercial potential:** Accessible party format and invitations; modest premium packs rather than an ambitious live service.

**Weakness and failure:** Could be a one-evening quiz novelty, and sensitive personal inference would be unwelcome. Kill if direct human guessing is equally entertaining or if agents produce bland consensus choices.

## 16. Counterspell

**Core idea and fantasy:** Invent compact rules for a spell and wield the resulting behavior in a constrained puzzle arena. Feel like a designer of strange but dependable powers.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans specify and demonstrate an intended transformation, then decide how to use it. An agent interprets the intention into an inspectable behavior using a fixed vocabulary of legal effects.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Describe a spell that returns dropped objects to the last stable surface. Test how the interpreter handles moving platforms, revise the definition, and use the accepted spell to cross an arena. Its validated behavior remains stable during the attempt.

**Why agents matter:** The expressive interpretation step opens more combinations than a conventional spell menu.

**Mastery and retention:** Discovering useful compositions, anticipating edge cases, and applying powers elegantly. Shared spell designs could extend replay.

**Social and commercial potential:** Strong short-video hook and a premium puzzle audience.

**Weakness and failure:** The agent is closer to a compiler than a continuing participant. If a node editor serves the same purpose, it fails the starting constraint. Park as a possible interaction mechanic, not the lead game.

## 17. Living Pursuit

**Core idea and fantasy:** Shape an agent adversary that stages challenges for another player while you play against theirs. Feel like both hunter and hunted in an evolving contest.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans play directly and set limited adversary preferences between rounds. Agents allocate threats, adapt routes, and pursue objectives under equal action budgets.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Build a pursuer that favors ambushes, then attempt an opponent's course. Midway through your run, its agent abandons an exposed ambush and attempts to close an alternate path. Escape, inspect what happened, and revise your own defender.

**Why agents matter:** The adversary adapts to current human behavior rather than only executing preplaced traps.

**Mastery and retention:** Evasion, anticipating adaptation, and authoring a coherent opposing style. Asynchronous challenges and rivals encourage rematches.

**Social and commercial potential:** Share chase replays; a premium action audience may be reachable without simultaneous matchmaking.

**Weakness and failure:** It contains two games with different mastery demands. Many adaptive adversaries need no external agent. Kill if a strong generic policy erases authored differences or if balancing both roles makes neither satisfying.

## 18. Unfinished Machines

**Core idea and fantasy:** Build an unusual body and collaborate with an agent that discovers how to operate it. Feel like an inventor whose creations develop surprising uses.

**Human and agent roles:** Humans choose morphology, components, terrain changes, and mission priorities. Agents learn or adapt locomotion and manipulation within the body's capabilities.

**Actual gameplay and fun:** Build a three-legged carrier with a long counterweight, test it on uneven ground, then modify the body when the agent discovers a useful swinging motion. The human designs a route and physically deploys supports during the mission.

**Why agents matter:** Adaptive control makes unfamiliar bodies usable without requiring players to program every movement.

**Mastery and retention:** Understanding embodied constraints and exploiting surprising movement. New components and terrain provide a long invention curve.

**Social and commercial potential:** Visually legible creations and surprising test clips; premium construction game with a plausible creator community.

**Weakness and failure:** Reliable control over arbitrary bodies is a major capability and compute risk. Instant excellent control may hide the agent and turn it into a conventional vehicle builder. Park until a bounded embodiment vocabulary offers reliable, fast adaptation.

| Teach | 1 | A companion's transferable approach |
| Coordinate and act | 2 | A jointly achieved expedition outcome |
| Construct a challenge | 3 | A situation others can interpret and solve |
| Improvise dramatically | 4 | A coherent performance |
| Infer and deceive socially | 5 | Correct identification of hidden roles or events |
| Investigate and argue | 6 | A defensible case |
| Alter developmental conditions | 7 | An adaptive lineage |
| Participate as a fan | 8 | A collectively shaped spectacle |
| Practice against adaptation | 9 | Personal execution skill |
| Bargain and authorize | 10 | A mutually contingent agreement |
| Prioritize and serve | 11 | A functioning operation under pressure |
| Build and detect decoys | 12 | A successful or defeated interpretation |
| Listen and perform music | 13 | An adaptive ensemble performance |
| Provision and await | 14 | An unfolding expedition history |
| Express and predict taste | 15 | An entertaining comparison with human preference |
| Define and wield rules | 16 | A stable new in-game power |
| Evade and shape an adversary | 17 | A contest against personally shaped pursuit |
| Design a body | 18 | A working, surprising machine |

The overlap between teaching, building, and training is intentional and exposed here. Their primary manipulated object differs: behavior in 1, environment in 3, developmental conditions in 7, and physical embodiment in 18. They should be merged only if one strengthens the other's core activity without multiplying the required game.
