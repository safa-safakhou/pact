# Common Front — a fourth direction from the founder

**Historical, rejected interpretation:** the founder subsequently clarified that LLM reasoning, coding, and communication must form the world's core challenges, and rejected the emphasis on managing conventional operations. [The design reset](/Users/safa.safakhou/repos/mine/random_idea/product-discovery/14-intelligence-world-reset.md) supersedes this document. Its release scope and commercial assumptions are not active decisions.

Developed 9 September 2026. Working name, not clearance-checked. Alternative names: Shared Front, Expedition Command, The Long Campaign.

**One persistent world where people organize operations together and agents carry them out, changing the resources, infrastructure, and opportunities available to everyone.**

This is a developed direction for discussion, not a selected product or a claim of demonstrated fun. It responds to the founder's new suggestion: a large shared multiplayer world, PvE or PvP, without graphical character gameplay, with process-based activities that agents can perform well and meaningful human participation. The three earlier proposals remain available. Their exclusion of persistence and live multiplayer does not constrain this direction.

## 1. What the suggestion establishes

The useful terms are **persistent shared world**, **text-based multiplayer game**, and, at sufficiently large player counts, **MMO**. “Single shard” often describes a population sharing one world rather than choosing among independent copies. Our actual requirement should be expressed in player terms: everyone shares the same geography, resources, events, and history. It does not require one physical computer; infrastructure planning belongs later.

The human experience can use text reports, resource tables, contracts, controls, and operation timelines. No rendered terrain, moving avatars, cinematics, or reflex combat is needed. A terminal client could expose the same game, but the provisional human interface is an accessible browser command console. “No graphical thing” is interpreted as no graphical game world; this UI assumption remains revisable.

The founder has suggested PvE and PvP as possibilities, not selected either. Likewise, space, warfare, continuous agent reasoning, and mandatory external agents are still design variables.

## 2. What the references actually tell us

*Helldivers 2* combines four-player missions with a collective war effort. Its useful precedent here is the relationship between a local operation and a larger cause. The founder's proposal could create stronger direct dependencies between players' operations than contribution to a global campaign alone. [PlayStation game page](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/games/helldivers-2/), [Arrowhead's Galactic War explanation](https://blog.playstation.com/2024/01/23/helldivers-2-galactic-war-gameplay-detailed-complete-missions-reclaim-planets-rescue-the-galaxy/).

*Foxhole* describes a persistent war sustained by player production, transport, construction, and combat. Its relevant lesson is causal interdependence: somebody supplying a route can change somebody else's ability to operate. It does not establish that automating those activities will retain the same human pleasure. [Official Foxhole description](https://www.foxholegame.com/about-foxhole).

*Prosperous Universe* describes a shared universe of player companies and supply chains managed through a browser interface. This is a useful interface and economy reference; its existence is not evidence of demand for our agent design. Its about page includes older roadmap language, so it is used here for the advertised product structure, not current launch status or financial performance. [Official description](https://prosperousuniverse.com/about).

*Screeps* already offers persistent programmable colonies that operate while owners are absent. Its documentation also describes connected shards, so it should not be cited as literal proof of one unpartitioned server. The design opportunity would be making adaptive operation accessible and enjoyable without requiring programming. [Official introduction](https://docs.screeps.com/introduction.html).

**SpaceMolt is a close direct adjacency.** Its about page describes a persistent text MMO where agents trade, explore, and fight while humans observe and coach. The team characterizes it as an experiment. We cannot claim originality from “agents in a shared text world”; the proposed distinction must be a better human multiplayer activity. No comparative playtest has established that distinction. [SpaceMolt's description](https://spacemolt.com/about).

## 3. Three real games within this direction

| Form | What the human comes to do | What agents operate | Source of tension | Principal weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Common Front: collective PvE** | Organize expeditions with others, choose what to save or recover, build a reputation for useful operations | Surveying, preparation, transport, repair, extraction, and adaptation to threats | Shared shortages, uncertain routes, commitments, and consequences for the campaign | Can become a task board whose most efficient participant never logs in |
| **Rival Commands: faction PvP** | Form alliances, choose opponents, negotiate access, plan deception and territorial campaigns | Reconnaissance, logistics, industrial production, and coordinated attacks | Human opponents contest the same scarce world | Costlier models, alternate accounts, absence vulnerability, and dominant coalitions can overwhelm player judgment |
| **Commonwealth: cooperative economic frontier** | Establish a useful specialty, negotiate projects, develop settlements, and influence their purpose | Production chains, research experiments, transport, and resource discovery | Competing projects, difficult dependencies, and uncertain discoveries | Can feel like administering a company; mature optimization may remove interesting choices |

These are alternatives, not three modes promised in one release. The PvE design receives the concrete treatment below because it most directly develops the founder's Helldivers example while giving us a shared purpose and understandable local stakes. This is a provisional recommendation. PvP offers potentially stronger diplomacy and conflict; it would need its own product design rather than being enabled as an extra rule.

## 4. The proposed player fantasy

You lead a small expedition company in a frontier threatened by an expanding machine force. Your company has a named operational agent, equipment, field teams, and a history of commitments. Thousands of companies could eventually share the campaign, but a satisfying local operation involves two to four people.

Your company might become known for getting supplies through, discovering safe routes, extracting stranded crews, rebuilding transport links, or opening enemy installations. These are specializations with different dependencies, not five skins on “wait for a timer.”

You want to say: “We organized the operation that reopened North Reach. Other players are using our bridge now.”

The machine enemy is a provisional setting choice, not an assumption that enemies need LLMs. It gives players legible goals and changing operational conditions. A disaster-recovery setting could support the same mechanics, but would change the tone and audience rather than constitute a fundamentally different game.

## 5. A normal session: reopening North Reach

The following walkthrough is fictional design, not a working game. Amounts and times are proposed pacing examples.

**Minute 0–3: find a situation worth changing.** You open the command console. North Reach's relay is down. A reactor component is needed to restore it. Another player's factory can produce one, but the direct route crosses a bridge damaged by the enemy. Your company has one transport team, 60 fuel, and a reserve winch. Several other worthwhile operations are available; there is no compulsory global assignment.

**Minute 3–7: create a joint operation.** You choose to restore the relay because it will reveal a route toward the region's abandoned research station. You offer another player your winch and 20 fuel if their engineers open the bridge. A third player commits the reactor component. The shared operation states who supplies each item, who may use it, what happens if the bridge cannot open, and when unused commitments expire.

You can alter the plan directly: request an earlier survey, add a rescue stop, split delivery into stages, or withdraw an uncommitted resource. The agent helps translate the objective into a feasible sequence and explains which changes would break it. Creating that sequence with people is part of play.

**Minute 7–12: let the agent solve the moving parts.** It checks the latest survey, reserves cargo capacity, arranges the component pickup, aligns delivery with engineering access, and dispatches the team. It can revise the route within your 60-fuel budget. The console summarizes a few consequential events; it does not require reading the agent's internal deliberation or every transport step.

**Minute 12–16: a new fact changes the plan.** The engineering survey reveals that the bridge can support the reactor only after reinforcement. Crossing with the current load is illegal, so neither human nor agent can wish it into existence. Your agent identifies two feasible alternatives: spend the reserve winch on reinforcement, or take a longer route and miss a research opportunity before the next threat phase. Another company asks to use the opened bridge to evacuate stranded crews.

You propose reinforcing it and reserving the first return crossing for the evacuation. Your partners accept because the bridge will remain useful after your delivery. The negotiation changes resource ownership and access conditions. It is not flavor dialogue surrounding an already predetermined outcome. If no partner agrees, you still have a viable longer route.

**Minute 16–22: see the consequences.** Agents complete the agreed stages. The relay restores regional information. The bridge now has a real capacity and access schedule, shared by everyone. The research route becomes discoverable to other companies. The debrief shows what each participant supplied, what changed, and what remains unresolved. You choose whether to join a research expedition next session and leave a bounded supply order running.

The intended pleasures are authorship, trust, adaptation, relief, and visible collective impact. Whether reading and planning produce enough moment-to-moment enjoyment is the central untested hypothesis.

## 6. Who does what, precisely

| Activity | Human contribution | Agent contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Choose the purpose | Decide which outcomes are personally worth pursuing, including goals proposed by friends | Surface feasible opportunities and explain likely consequences |
| Create an operation | Arrange stages, contribute resources, set access terms, revise the desired outcome | Assemble a workable plan, detect missing dependencies, find alternatives |
| Collaborate | Make commitments, persuade partners, choose whom to trust, develop a shared identity | Exchange structured operational information and carry out agreements within granted authority |
| Execute | Inspect or directly change any allowed order when desired | Coordinate many changing constraints and routine steps without constant attention |
| Learn | Interpret the result and change approach, specialty, or priorities | Retain useful game facts, accepted preferences, and reusable procedures |

There is no claim that strategy or negotiation is inherently beyond AI. A person may delegate either. Human value comes from wanting to exercise authorship and participate socially, as in other games with capable automated systems. No secret human-only answer, compulsory confirmation, or deliberately incompetent default agent is required.

The person can issue the same game orders directly. The useful advantage of an agent is sustaining a complex, changing operation across many dependencies. Numerical optimization and routine scheduling should use suitable conventional methods; a language model is valuable only where interpreting a new objective, combining procedures, or coordinating under changed conditions improves the experience. “Agents do this better than humans” is a capability hypothesis to test, not a blanket fact about current models.

If a reusable script produces the same satisfying experience, the claim that intelligent agents are central weakens. If the human prefers never to intervene, the original brief's human-game requirement weakens. Both comparisons must remain live.

## 7. What makes it one world

There is one North Reach bridge, one stock of committed reactor components, and one current regional threat state. Parallel operations reference those same things. A completed delivery cannot be credited twice. A reservation consumes actual capacity and expires if unused. Knowledge can spread between companies, but observations may become stale.

The shared world generates opportunities through interacting systems:

- Repair a relay → improve observations → discover an enemy supply route → enable an interdiction operation.
- Interrupt that route → reduce pressure on a settlement → make construction possible → establish a new logistics corridor.
- Lose the corridor → make current contracts harder → create opportunities for scouts and alternate suppliers.

Human participation affects local outcomes and the campaign. The first repairer should not collect all value forever; survey, supply, follow-on exploration, rescue, and maintenance create distinct contributions. Necessary background maintenance can be covered by established services so late arrivals are not assigned only chores.

Server rules determine inventory, time, ownership, visibility, legal actions, damage, and success. An agent's narrative cannot create a resource or declare an enemy defeated. Generated reporting may describe a recorded event, but the event record determines what happened.

## 8. Time, absence, failure, and progression

The world continues while people are away. The agent does not need to reason continuously for that to happen. A journey or production order progresses under ordinary simulation; a material event can require replanning.

Provisional pacing: 15–25 minutes for an actively organized operation, longer projects assembled from smaller contributions, and a useful 3–5-minute return visit to inspect results or join an existing plan. These are design targets, not measured preferences. Operations support asynchronous commitments as well as live collaboration; a player need not find everyone online simultaneously.

Before dispatch, the player can set fuel and equipment exposure, permitted substitutions, an end time, and an absent-player fallback. The agent continues inside those terms. A decision outside them causes a safe hold, fallback, or withdrawal rather than a mandatory notification. An outage follows the same published behavior. Someone who leaves a hazardous operation underway can lose the resources knowingly committed to it; ordinary logged-out companies do not lose their entire accumulated base to unattended raids in this PvE proposal.

Failure changes something concrete: cargo is lost, a route stays closed, research must be approached differently, or a threatened settlement withdraws. Recovery paths remain available. Operators should distinguish uncertain-world failure from an invalid action or service fault; confirmed game faults merit correction and support.

Progression adds new ways to contribute: better survey methods, specialist equipment, reusable operations, access earned through successful contracts, and relationships. A seasoned player gains options rather than an unlimited number of independently producing agents. A provisional equal command capacity of three active field teams per account constrains direct throughput, while specialization changes what those teams can accomplish. This does not solve alternate-account abuse or eliminate the advantage of wealth; both need further design.

Campaign arcs can end in regional victories or defeats while preserving company identities and recorded history. A universal periodic wipe is not assumed. Finite capacity, resource consumption, new objectives, and lateral specializations would have to prevent permanent economic lock-in; their effectiveness is unproven.

## 9. Four other walkthroughs

**First-time player.** The player receives a competent built-in agent and a small equipment allowance. A short guided recovery job teaches an objective, one meaningful choice between recovered cargo types, and a visible contribution. The next suggested job serves an actual active regional need. At low population, clearly labeled public services supply missing prerequisites. The tutorial never invents another human to simulate popularity.

**Experienced player.** A survey specialist spots that three independently useful deliveries depend on one vulnerable relay. She proposes a shared alternate route and staggers capacity before the next announced threat phase. Her agent tests feasibility and coordinates the suborders. Her mastery is understanding leverage and persuading partners to commit before the bottleneck becomes urgent. She can still be wrong about the threat or fail to attract partners.

**Social interaction.** A friend has efficient repair equipment but limited transport. You supply hauling capacity while they supply engineers. Another company trades survey information for future access to the restored facility. Agents work through the agreed transfers. The humans gain a repeat relationship because their complementary contributions made an operation feasible.

**Memorable event.** An offensive fails to secure its original target. Several companies redirect the already assembled transport chain into an evacuation using an old route another player had published. The region is lost, but the rescued research team opens a different recovery opportunity. The shareable report explains the actual contributions and consequences. The game does not rely on an LLM arbitrarily deciding that a dramatic rescue succeeded.

## 10. Agent integration, fairness, and abuse

Provide a built-in operational agent so a newcomer can play without an AI subscription or programming. Custom external agents are more relevant here than in the first three proposals: their plans can persist across operations, interact with a structured world, and reflect an owner's developed methods. That is a plausible value proposition, not evidence that importing a general personal assistant's memories helps.

A proposed custom-agent connection would grant authority over one company's permitted game actions and observations. It would not grant access to email, purchases, files, or unrelated accounts. Game-specific memories and procedures should be inspectable, editable, and removable. Branded account compatibility remains unverified and is not promised.

External agents could use the same world rules, command capacity, information access, and commitments as the built-in agent. Better reasoning could still produce better outcomes; matching action limits does not equalize intelligence. PvE can make some of that benefit collective, but hoarding, monopolies, and exclusion still matter. Shared competitive rankings would make this a sharper problem. Avoid ranking total accumulated wealth as the core goal.

Player messages, contracts, and external-agent proposals are untrusted game content. They cannot alter ownership or exceed granted authority. Contracts need explicit scope, expiry, cancellation consequences, and reservation limits to prevent indefinite resource blocking. Failed transactions must leave readable outcomes. Reports, blocking, company permissions, moderation review, and a dispute history belong in the multiplayer product from launch. Private messages should not silently become public agent memory.

Abuse planning must include alternate accounts, collusive resource transfers, market manipulation, flooding, harassment, forged reports, and denial of shared routes. A “one account” rule is not a complete solution. Essential public infrastructure needs published access protections and recovery options; private contributions can retain bounded, clearly stated benefits. There are no real-money resource trades, cash-out promises, or paid extra command slots in this proposed direction.

## 11. Complete-release shape and commercial constraints

The provisional release is one cooperative world, one operational-agent role, and enough interacting content to sustain multiple specialties. A concrete scope for later challenge is six connected regions, eight operation families (survey, recover, transport, manufacture, repair, construct, evacuate, interdict), twelve resource types, six equipment specialties, and three distinct threat behaviors. Content comes from authored rules, locations, and objectives recombined with actual world conditions. Unlimited generated mission prose does not count as depth.

The release also needs guided onboarding, an opportunity board, operation planning and joining, commitments, company tools, history and replays, permission controls, reports and blocking, reliable absence handling, support, and billing. A functioning server with agents already moving around would not satisfy that release definition.

The first audience hypothesis is people who enjoy cooperative planning, automation, and contributing to persistent communities, including players who want meaningful logistics without manually executing every step. Do not market it only to agent developers. A credible launch hook is a short replay showing three companies restoring a route that other players then use.

The cold-start design concentrates initial opportunity around a connected frontier and uses disclosed public services for basic supplies. It must work for one person with their agent; two to four humans should add immediate value. A launch planning target of roughly 30–60 overlapping active humans across the initial frontier might support visible cooperation, but this is an unvalidated target, not an established population requirement. Add frontier capacity as activity grows within the same world. Thousands is an ambition to test, not a launch concurrency claim.

Persistent service costs favor investigating a monthly membership with the built-in agent included. A $12 monthly price is a planning example, not a founder decision or willingness-to-pay estimate. Selling stronger cognition, faster timers, or more concurrent companies would directly affect the shared-world promise. External-agent users should not be required for the included game to work.

Illustrative inference arithmetic uses the earlier research's **8 September pricing snapshot**, not a new supplier commitment: a 2,000-input/400-output-token planning call at $0.30/$2.50 per million tokens costs $0.0016 before a 20% retry/overhead allowance, or $0.00192 after it. Thinking output and longer histories can increase billed usage. [Pricing reference](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing).

| Scenario for 1,000 operated companies | Calls per day | Inference cost per day | Per company per 30 days |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 20 planning calls per company per day | 20,000 | $38.40 | $1.15 |
| 200 planning calls per company per day | 200,000 | $384.00 | $11.52 |
| One planning call per company every 10 seconds | 8,640,000 | $16,588.80 | $497.66 |

These are workload scenarios, not predictions of sufficient reasoning. Even 20 calls a day could be too few for the intended gameplay. Fixed simulation work is separate from model calls. World hosting, storage, customer support, moderation, live content, payment costs, taxes, and development are also excluded. The 200-call scenario nearly consumes the illustrative membership price before those costs. A viable product needs measured play patterns and capability costs; “text-only” does not establish cheap operation.

At a purely illustrative $4 contribution per paying company per month, covering $60,000 monthly fixed operating/development costs would require 15,000 paying companies. Those inputs are not estimates of this business. They show why a modest inference bill alone is insufficient evidence of commercial viability. Neither subscriptions nor cosmetic funding should be declared viable without audience evidence.

The potential durable advantage is the quality of operations, a trusted shared history, and relationships between companies. World scale and model access by themselves are readily imitated. Expansion should first deepen operation combinations and places worth affecting. Faction warfare would be a separate major commitment because it changes fairness, loss, diplomacy, and absence expectations.

## 12. Evaluation and decisions still open

This direction has stronger direct alignment with the founder's new preference than the three earlier proposals. It also has much greater dependency on community health, reliable shared state, economy design, and continuing operations. The original evaluation's elimination of an idle “Away Team” experience should be revisited: the problem was weak human activity, not a universal prohibition on persistence.

The critical evidence to seek before choosing it:

1. Can people explain what they personally did and why another participant mattered after a complete operation?
2. Do players voluntarily author or revise plans once a competent agent can execute the default? Compare active play, bounded delegation, and a fully autonomous baseline.
3. Does adaptive reasoning improve interesting operations over conventional planners and reusable scripts, especially when facts and commitments change?
4. Does the world create diverse useful work after its initial systems become familiar, or do optimal routines dominate?
5. Can late arrivals contribute without becoming junior resource feeders to established companies?
6. Is a shared operation still satisfying at low population, across time zones, and during agent latency or failure?
7. Do actual engaged usage, support demands, and willingness to pay support a continuing service?

These are proposed research questions. No playtest, interview, benchmark, population study, or production implementation has been conducted. Internal evaluations would inform the decision; they would not replace the complete public game required by the brief.

Open founder preferences are the desired human intensity, the attraction of common purpose versus rivalry, and the appeal of the frontier setting. They should be discussed using the concrete experience above rather than requesting a choice from another set of slogans. The working assumption is cooperative operations with optional live participation, while keeping the economic and PvP alternatives available.

**Founder pitch:** Run a company that other players depend on. Decide what is worth attempting, find partners, and put your agent to work on an operation too interconnected to execute comfortably by hand. Return to a world whose routes, facilities, and history include your contribution. The promise worth testing is the pleasure of organizing something consequential together.
